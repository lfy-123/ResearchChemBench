Reply: We sincerely appreciate your thoughtful comments, which highlight an important aspect of
our study. While the in-situ experiments indeed provide strong evidence of thermally activated
mass exchange within the bulk, our key objective was to go beyond bulk observations and establish
how these processes couple to the surface in the NiAl system at elevated temperatures. By
integrating complementary experimental techniques with DFT modeling, we were able to capture
not only the bulk mass exchange but also its manifestation at the surface through phase segregation
and charge transfer phenomena. We believe this bulk-surface coupling is central to understanding
the high-temperature behavior of NiAl and represents a key contribution of our work. Please find
a point-by-point response to the queries raised.

1. The question arises about the complete novelty of the work? There are several studies
already existing on segregation of Ni and γ^, formation in β-NiAl system and their
mechanism. (Some suggestion – Marino et. Al. Phys. Rev. B 78, 184105, etc.). If I am not

Reply: We sincerely thank you for raising this important point regarding the novelty of our work
and providing insightful references. You are correct to note that seminal computational studies,
such as those by Marino and Carter, have elegantly modeled diffusion mechanisms in β-NiAl. We
agree that our manuscript should explicitly position itself in relation to this foundational work.

Our study was never intended to question these established mechanisms but rather to build upon
them by providing the first direct experimental demonstration of a consequent phenomenon that,
to our knowledge, has not been predicted or observed: the spontaneous surface segregation of Ni
and precipitation of γ'-Ni3Al on stoichiometric NiAl under inert conditions.

We have  significantly  revised  the  discussion  sections  to  better frame  this  contribution,
incorporating the suggested and related literature. The key novel aspects of our work are:

1.  Experimental validation of a new phenomenon: While Marino et. al.’s DFT calculations (Phys.
   Rev. B 78, 184105) and subsequent work (Acta Materialia 58, 2726) predict comparable bulk
    diffusion rates for Ni and Al, they do not preclude kinetic segregation under a thermal gradient.
   Our multimodal experimental results directly show that Ni preferentially segregates to the
    surface upon annealing, forming Ni3Al precipitates. This provides a critical experimental
   benchmark for future computational models of surface and near-surface kinetics.
2.  Distinction from oxidation-driven segregation: While the formation of Ni3Al during the
    oxidation of NiAl has been observed (Zhang et al., Acta Materialia 59 (2011), 5905), it is
   understood to result from the preferential consumption of Al during the growth of the Al2O3

Reply: Thank you for this insightful comment, which allows us to clarify the compelling, albeit
indirect, quantitative evidence for vacancies in our study. We agree that directly imaging single
vacancies at high temperature is exceptionally challenging. Therefore, our approach relied on a
multi-faceted methodology to build a quantitative case for the presence of vacancies and their
preferential nature.

Our presented evidence is threefold:

1.  In-situ high-temperature HRTEM imaging and diffraction evidence: The key evidence
   comes from our in-situ HRTEM experiments at 800 °C (Fig. 5f). The appearance of unique
    superlattice reflections (Fig. 5(g, h)) is a direct signature of a periodic structural modification
    in the bulk. Simulated diffraction patterns based on atomic models incorporating ordered
   vacancies show excellent agreement with the experimental FFT patterns, quantitatively
    supporting vacancy ordering at the annealing temperature.
2.  Post-cooling HAADF-STEM evidence: Upon cooling, the equilibrium concentration of
   thermal vacancies decreases, leading to their aggregation. Bands of lower intensity in the
  HAADF-STEM image (Fig. 5k) are a direct manifestation of these vacancy clusters. While
    this is qualitative, it clearly shows the aftermath and tendency of vacancies to form in the bulk
3.  Quantitative intensity profiling demonstrating preferential Ni vacancies: To directly
    address your request for quantification, we performed intensity line profile analysis on the
  HAADF-STEM image (Fig. 5k). In the B2-ordered NiAl structure, the intensity of an atomic
   column in HAADF is directly proportional to the atomic number (Z-contrast), allowing for
    distinguishing Ni (Z=28) and Al (Z=13) columns:
   • Our analysis (Fig. 5l) of randomly selected {110} planes (yellow and cyan rectangles, Fig.
      5k) reveals alternating high-intensity (Ni) and low-intensity (Al) columns, as expected for
    B2 NiAl.
   •  Crucially, Ni columns show significant intensity variations (yellow arrows, Fig. 5l). This is
       direct quantitative measure of a reduced number of atoms in those columns,  i.e., the
      presence of Ni vacancies.
   •  In contrast, the intensity of Al columns is far more uniform, indicating a significant lower
      concentration of Al vacancies.
   •  This intensity line profile analysis provides direct evidence for a preferential Ni vacancy
      population, consistent with the observed Ni surface segregation phenomena and subsequent
      Ni3Al precipitation.

In  summary,  while  single-vacancy  imaging  is  challenging,  the  combination  of  in-situ
diffractograms, HAADF-STEM intensity analysis, and simulated models provides a convergent
body of evidence for preferential Ni vacancy formation driving the surface segregation phenomena.
We have amended the manuscript to clearly present this chain of evidence by adding the following
passages at multiple places:
