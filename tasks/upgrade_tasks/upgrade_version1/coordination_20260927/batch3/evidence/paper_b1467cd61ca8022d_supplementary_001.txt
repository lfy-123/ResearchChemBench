Reviewer comments:

Reviewer #1

(Remarks to the Author)
In this paper, the authors evaluate the electrochemical properties, solvation environment, and performance of asymmetric
fluorinated solvents for lithium-ion batteries. The primary focus is to improve the low temperature operation of liquid
electrolytes for lithium metal batteries through fluorinated solvents. They also performed theoretical calculations to offer
possible explanations for the observed electrochemical trends and behavior. They identified 3,3,3-trifluoropropyl-1-methyl
ether (TFPM) as the top performing solvent for low temperature operation with a 2M LiFSI salt.

My primary concern with this paper is the lack of novelty for their research approach. There are countless papers and
reviews describing the enhanced low temperature performance of fluorinated and/or asymmetric solvents for lithium ion
batteries in this and similar journals. These reports use a similar approach to this work by understanding how to control the
solvation/coordination of lithium with fluorinated solvents to impact factors such as ionic conductivity, kinetics, oxidation
stability, and stable SEI formation. This also includes prior work by the authors from 2023 (Angew. Chem. Int. Ed. 2023, 62,
e202218151) in which they look at a structurally similar 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoro-3-methoxypropane (TFMP) solvent. They use a
7:1 mix of TFMP with DME with 1 m LiFSI. They showed cycling at -40 oC with NMC811 for 100 cycles with the electrolyte
in coin cell format. This work and general conclusions are very similar to this current study.

In the current work, the authors also present a thorough characterization of the system across multiple time and length scales
with a variety of analytical techniques as they previously did for TFMP from 2023. They show improvement in coin cell
cycling at -40 oC with NMC811 for 200 cycles with minimal loss in capacity for the TFPM solvent compared to the TFMP
solvent. They also expand the practical use of the TFPM solvent by demonstration of 6.0 Ah pouch cells that can operate at
low temperature (-40 oC) with good capacity retention and coulombic efficiency. They also demonstrate -60 oC operation in
a coin cell with good performance for 150 cycles. The use of lean electrolyte conditions and low N/P ratio in these cells and
comparison of its ambient and low temperature performance with current literature highlights the potential utility of the
electrolyte solvent. However, is this enough to overcome the lack of novelty in their approach compared to prior work from
their group and others which should be addressed? If addressed, it could be accepted. Aside from this issue, the following
points should also be considered:

1. Figure 2 only shows the coordination environment for a single lithium cation and solvent molecule. While this highlights
the point the authors are trying to make regarding the differences in energies and bond distances for Li-O and F-O, it does
not provide a real picture of the solvation environment. How do the bond distances and coordination environment change as
the number of solvent molecules is increased? Additionally, where is the anion in the solvation structure? This can be
extracted from the MD simulations and further optimized via DFT to show the “true” Li+ coordination in the solvent. This will
provide a much more accurate representation of the bonding energies and distances in the electrolyte system.

2. Why was LiFSI chosen rather than other electrolyte salts (i.e. LiTFSI, LiClO, LiPF6)? Comment on if you would expect
similar or different behavior for other common Li salts with these solvents.

3. Was the impact of LiFSI concentration on any of the electrochemical properties evaluated since it appears that only 2M
solution was evaluated?

4. While the radar chart is a visually nice figure (7f), it does not appear that there was a true qualitative methodology for

=== PDF PAGE 2 ===
assessing the designed electrolytes since 4 out of the 5 aspects show the electrolyte has the ideal properties. This makes it
appear to readers as if these aspects have been solved, which I do not think that is the case since it is only a qualitative
assessment. This could be deceiving to readers and should have some qualitative figures of merit for the rankings. If not, it
should be removed from the main text.

5. I think there I a typo in line 275, should it be “PM, TFPM, and TFBM” rather than “BM, TFPM, and TFBM” since the BM
shows Al corrosion in Figure S21b while the PM, TFPM, and TFBM electrolytes do not.

6. There are also multiple typos and grammar errors in the paper with the following words misused: line 40 “demanding”; line
46 “wakening”; line 88 “enhances”; line 111 “facial”; lines117-118 sentence is very difficult to understand; and line 349
“slighter”



Reviewer #2

(Remarks to the Author)
Zehao et al. introduced a promising approach for screening electrolyte solvents for lithium-metal batteries operating under
ultra-low temperatures and high voltages using the RESPO–μ principle. As a result, the fluorinated ethers TFPM and TFBM
demonstrate superior electrochemical performance compared to their non-fluorinated counterparts, PM and BM. However, it
should be noted that a conceptually similar strategy involving rational solvent tuning was previously proposed in a diethyl
ether (DEE)-based system by Zhiao et al. (Nat. Energy 7, 94–106, 2022), where improvements in oxidation stability and low-
temperature performance were achieved by manipulating the solvation structure. While the present manuscript is thorough,
methodically executed, and well-organized, the central concept remains insufficiently substantiated. How solvents with
moderate dipole moment (μ) contribute to interphase stability? This mechanism warrants further clarification through either
direct or indirect experimental validation. Given the high standards of Nature Communications, the authors are encouraged
to address the following concerns before the manuscript can be considered for publication.
Some detailed comments are attached below.
1. In the 7Li NMR spectra, the observed downfield shift (de-shielding) of Li+ in LiFSI/BM and LiFSI/PM suggests weaker
solvation compared to LiFSI/TFPM and LiFSI/TFBM. However, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations indicate that TFPM
and TFBM electrolytes exhibit highly similar solvation structures, as stated in the manuscript: Li+(FSI−)3.03(TFPM)0.99, and
Li+(FSI−)2.93(TFBM)1.08 . How can the significantly lower de-solvation energy observed for LiFSI/TFPM be rationalized
relative to the other systems?
2. In both ¹⁷O and ¹⁹F NMR spectra, the addition of Li salt results in an upfield shift for oxygen and a downfield shift for
fluorine. This suggests that fluorine acts as an electron donor, whereas oxygen functions as an electron acceptor. The author
should consider the distinct electronic roles of oxygen and fluorine in the FSI⁻ anion. Particularly since LiFSI accounts for
approximately 75% of the coordination environment.
3. In Figure 3a-d, the TFBM has significantly more AGGs/CIPs compared to TFPM. Why does it not yield better Echem
performance?
4. 99.3% overall Coulombic efficiency (CE) in a Li/Cu cell is not unprecedented for LiFSI-based systems. For example,
values of 99.4% have been reported in 1 M LiFSI in BTFE/DME (Energy Environ. Sci., 2022, 15, 1647–1658), and up to
99.7% in 1.2 M LiFSI/F5DEE (Nat. Energy, 2022, 7, 94–106). At a minimum, the authors should provide a fair comparison
with the current state-of-the-art.
5. If dissolution is indeed an issue in the TFBM system, can the difference in SEI composition be identified via XPS? Based
on the current data (Figure 5a and S14), the SEI appears similar in both the TFBM and TFPM systems. How, then, can the
authors substantiate their claim that dissolution leads to the growth of redundant SEI?
6. The high-voltage stability of fluorinated solvents is not surprising and has also been reported in mentioned Nature Energy
paper. Fluorination lowers the HOMO energy level of the molecules, thereby enhancing their oxidative stability. However,
how does solvent modification help prevent Al corrosion typically induced by LiFSI?
7. What is the extent of gas evolution in TFBM and TFPM cells during cycling at 4.4 V?



Reviewer #3

(Remarks to the Author)
Electrolyte solvent engineering could tune both the SEI structure and chemistry, which has been considered as a critical and
pragmatic approach to enable high performance Li-metal batteries. Unfortunately, the lack of solvent screening principle has
hindered their efficient development. In this work, the authors innovatively proposed a solvent screening principle based on
unique RESPO-μ dual-descriptors to enable high-energy LMBs at ultra-low temperatures. Furthermore, the authors design
and synthesize a new class of weakly solvating fluorinated ethers to validate the effectiveness of this RESPO–μ screening
principle. The work provides valuable insights into electrolyte design to enhances electrochemical kinetics and reversibility
simultaneously, achieving an unprecedented high energy density of 345.3 Wh kg−1 at −40°C. Therefore, from my
perspective, this manuscript is very interesting and should be very helpful to the field that is rapidly evolving recently. I would
enthusiastically recommend publication of this work after the following minor issues are properly addressed.

1. In the research of solvent descriptors, researchers have proposed numbers of parameters as criteria for solvent screening,
among which electrostatic potential is also a very important screening parameter. What are the advantages of the RESPO
parameter proposed by the authors compared to electrostatic potential?

2. The designed TFPM electrolyte presented good capacity retention and cycling stability at both room temperature and low

=== PDF PAGE 3 ===
temperature. Has the author explored the rate performance at low temperatures with TFPM electrolyte?

3. Among the series of fluorinated ether molecules designed, the formation of a hexagonal coordination structure can
significantly improve the performance of batteries, especially at ultra-low temperature. What influence does this configuration
have on the lithium deposition and kinetics at low temperatures?

4. The solvation of TFPM and TFBM is very similar, and the composition of SEI is almost the same at room temperature.
However, there is a significant difference in impedance between these two at low temperatures（−40 and −60°C）, and
there is also a significant difference in battery performance. What is the main affecting factor?

Authors’ Response: We would like to thank the reviewer for his/her useful comments

and suggestions to further improve our manuscript. But we dissent from the reviewer

that a work lacks novelty if something related to it has been studied, regardless of what

it achieves and how important it is. However, please be aware that, for the LiCO2

cathode material alone (discovered in 1980), there are many papers published on the

Nature, Science and Nature sister journals in the past 10 years. Similarly, fluoroethylene

carbonate (FEC) was first identified as solvent for graphite anode at 1999, they are

many works investigated its effect on other types of anodes, such as silicon and lithium

metal anodes, many of which have been published on Nature sister journals, Angew.

Chem., JACS and PANS. Therefore, we certainly can’t conclude that a work lacks

novelty if something related to it has been studied, as a matter of fact, we should

look into what it achieves and how important it is.

For reviewer’s concern on the novelty of our work, we would like to emphasize the

novelty in the following several aspects:

First, as pointed out by the Reviewer, there are indeed many papers describing the

enhanced low temperature performance of fluorinated and/or asymmetric solvents for

lithium-ion batteries, which was realized by controlling the solvation/coordination of
Li+ with fluorinated solvents to promote ionic conductivity, kinetics, oxidation stability,

and stable SEI formation, including our previous study (Angew. Chem. Int. Ed., 2023,

62, e202218151). However, most of these studies were realized by employing multiple

salts/solvents to compensate their individual drawbacks. Achieving fast kinetics,

superior interphase stability, and uniform Li deposition simultaneously in a single-

salt single-solvent system is extremely challenging, with only very few reports up

to now. To be specific, there is one very recent paper reported the operation of Li-metal

pouch-cell at low temperature of −20 °C using single-salt single-solvent electrolyte

(Joule, 2025, 9, 101823). While in our study, a Li-metal pouch-cell was demonstrated
to deliver an unprecedentedly high energy density of 345.3 Wh kg−1 over 40 cycles at

−40 °C. Based on the dramatically different electrolyte system, namely multiple-

solvent vs. single-solvent, one should NOT conclude that our work lacks novelty.

=== PDF PAGE 8 ===
Second, recent electrolyte solvent design based on weakening the solvation ability has

shown great potential, however, the lack of solvent screening principle has hindered

their efficient development. In this study, we innovatively proposed a guideline for

rational electrolyte solvent screening, where the restrained electrostatic potential of the

coordinated O (RESPO) was proposed as one descriptor to quantify the solvation effect,

followed by introducing the dipole moment (μ) as correction descriptor to optimize

solvents’ sensitivity to the electric fields and interphase stability. Under the guidance of

this RESPO–μ screening principle, a new class of asymmetric fluorinated ethers was

rationally designed, of which the 3,3,3-trifluoropropyl-1-methyl ether (TFPM) with

moderate RESPO and μ was identified to mediate a stable six-membered chelating
structure with Li+, significantly promoting Li kinetics and reversibility from 30 to

−60 °C. This study provides a highly efficient principle for rational electrolyte solvent

screening, opens up new frontiers in electrolyte design towards practical high-energy

LMBs under extreme temperatures, demonstrating that our study has both scientific

novelty and importance.

Third, as commented by the Reviewer, the screened TFPM electrolyte exhibits much

superior performance compared to previous literatures. Specifically, (1) For the Li||Cu

cell, TFPM electrolyte demonstrates ultra-stable cycling for over 850 cycles with a high

average CE of 99.3%, representing the pioneering work for “single salt single solvent”
electrolytes reported thus far at relatively high current densities of 1.0 mA cm−2

(Supplementary Table 2); (2) for Li-metal coin-cells with high areal capacity and low

N/P ratio, the cycling life advantage for TFPM electrolyte is very obvious at low-

temperature (≤ −20 °C) in comparison with other reported electrolytes (Supplementary

Table 4); (3) for the Li-metal pouch-cells, the cell using TFPM electrolyte delivers an
unprecedentedly high energy density of 345.3 Wh kg−1 over 40 cycles at −40 °C, which

stands out in terms of the highest energy density and longest cycles in comparison with

the reported studies at low temperatures (≤ −20 °C) (Supplementary Table 7), validating

the superiority of our study.

In summary, our study overcomes the long-standing challenges of Li compatibility

and electrochemical kinetics, and provides a highly efficient principle for rational

electrolyte solvent screening. This leads to an unprecedentedly high energy density of
345.3 Wh kg−1 over 40 cycles at −40 °C for Li-metal pouch-cell, which is unattainable

for previous electrolyte design and can be expanded to other metal batteries. Therefore,

our work has both scientific novelty and importance, and will be of significant interest

to the broad readership of Nat. Commun. in both scientific and industrial arena. The

significant progress, as well as the importance of high-energy and low-temperature

batteries in our society, warrants the publication of our manuscript in Nat. Commun.

Comment #2: Why was LiFSI chosen rather than other electrolyte salts (i.e. LiTFSI,

=== PDF PAGE 11 ===
LiClO4, LiPF6)? Comment on if you would expect similar or different behavior for other

common Li salts with these solvents.

Authors’ Response: Thanks for your insightful comment. Li salt not only dominates

the ion transport behavior in the bulk electrolyte, but also largely affects the solid

electrolyte interphase (SEI) components on the anode, which may result in vastly

different electrochemical performance of LMBs. LiFSI exhibits superior hydrolysis

resistant, high conductivity, and good solubility in ether solvents. While combined with
weakly solvating ether solvents, the reduced Li+–solvent coordination will not only
promote the de-solvation process but also intensify the Li+–FSI− pairing to facilitate the

formation of anion-derived inorganic-rich SEI  at a regular concentration, which

enhances the electrochemical kinetics and reversibility simultaneously, especially for

LMBs. Therefore, the combination of LiFSI-ether electrolytes has been widely adopted
and studied for high-energy density LMBs.[1-5]

As for the other Li salts mentioned by the Reviewer, (1) LiTFSI has a similar structure

and elemental compositions to the LiFSI, which possesses even better thermal and
moisture stability than LiFSI. Nevertheless, the larger volume of TFSI− anion and lower

conductivity  of LiTFSI  limits  its  kinetics  at  high-rate  conditions  or  ultra-low

temperatures; (2) LiClO4 does NOT contain F element and fails to contribute inorganic

LiF, of which the LiF has been recognized as an excellent SEI component to promotes

reversible Li plating/stripping. Furthermore, the high oxidation state of Cl (+7) raises

serious safety concerns; (3) LiPF6 is the most widely used and mature Li salt in

commercial Li-ion batteries. However,  it suffers from poor thermal and moisture

stability as well as limited solubility in ether solvents.

To comprehensively address the Reviewer’s comments, we extended our study to assess

the properties of TFPM with different Li salts. First, as shown in the following Figure

R3, LiFSI presents a higher max solubility (i.e., 5.9 M) than that of LiTFSI (i.e., 5.1

M), LiClO4 (i.e., 3.8 M), and LiPF6 (i.e., 1.6 M) in TFPM solvent, which contributes to

the sufficient ionization of LiFSI at the applied concentration (2 M) and avoid salt

precipitation at ultra-low temperatures. Second, the physical properties of TFPM

solvent with different Li salts at 2 M (expect 1.5 M for LiPF6) were also measured

(Figure R4). Among these four TFPM electrolytes, 2 M LiFSI/TFPM electrolyte

emerges as the optimal candidate for practical application due to its highest ionic

conductivity, relatively lower density and viscosity. Third, to show the universality of

=== PDF PAGE 12 ===
TFPM solvent with different salts in improving the electrochemical performance, 50

μm Li||4.0 mAh cm−2 NMC811 cell using 2 M LiTFSI/TFPM electrolyte was also

evaluated (Figure R5), which exhibits similarly good performance as that using 2 M

LiFSI/TFPM electrolyte. However, the 2 M LiTFSI/TFPM electrolyte delivers inferior

rate capability and low-temperature performance.

Therefore, the LiFSI salt remains the best choice in this study.

Comment #3: Was the impact of LiFSI concentration on any of the electrochemical

properties evaluated since it appears that only 2 M solution was evaluated?

Authors’ Response: Thanks for your comment. Prior to conducting the comprehensive

study, we actually conduct preliminary evaluations of the electrolytes with different

concentrations. For  the  conventional  Li-ion  batteries,  the  concentration  of  the

electrolyte was normally controlled at ~ 1 M to achieve a balance between ionic

conductivity,  viscosity, and  interfacial  stability.  Increasing  the  electrolyte  salt

concentration could effectively reduce the solvent molecules while extensively recruit
anions within the Li+ inner solvation sheath, leading to a high anodic stability as well

as an anion-derived inorganic-rich SEI with better Li cyclability, but at a cost of high

viscosity and cost.

Therefore, we first evaluated the Li||Cu cells using four electrolytes at a conventional

LiFSI concentration of 1 M. As shown in the following Figure R6, the cell using four

electrolytes all demonstrate high CEs of ≥ 98.5% with the 1 M LiFSI/TFPM electrolyte

being the highest of 99.5%. Next, to optimize the electrolyte performance, we further

=== PDF PAGE 14 ===
studied the ionic conductivity of TFPM system at different LiFSI concentration (Figure

R7). The  ionic  conductivity  of LiFSI/TFPM  electrolyte demonstrated a  linear

correlation with concentration at low concentrations, followed by a saturation turning

point upon exceeding 2 M. Furthermore, the 2 M LiFSI/TFPM electrolyte also

Authors’ Response: We would like to thank the reviewer for his/her comments and

insightful suggestions on our manuscript. We have addressed all comments below and

revised the manuscript accordingly.

Dipole moment (μ) reflects the unevenness of molecular charge distribution and is an

important parameter for studying the behavior of molecule under the electric field. Li

et al. compared a series of solvents with different μ and found that solvents with higher

μ tend to preferentially adsorb on the surface of Li-metal anode, thereby affecting the
electrochemical performance of the SEI.[6] Liu et al. reported an asymmetric Li salt,

lithium 1,1,1-trifluoro-N-[2-[2-(2-methoxyethoxy)ethoxy)]ethyl] methanesulfonamide

(LiFEA) that possesses a pseudo-crown ether-like anion, with a large molecular dipole,

which allows for self-cleaning of unfavorable organic components in the SEI during
cycling (Nat. Energy, 2023, 8, 934).[7] However, excessively high molecular dipole

could also dissolve out the desired inorganic components, thus reducing the stability of

SEI. Later on, they carefully designed and synthesized another Li salt, lithium 2-[2-(2-

methoxy  ethoxy)ethoxy]ethanesulfonyl(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (LiETFSI)

with suitable molecular dipole, which rationalized this self-cleaning behavior and
further improved its compatibility towards Li-metal anode.[8] Therefore, the μ of solvent

=== PDF PAGE 20 ===
must be regulated carefully to balance its ability to absorb and stabilize the interphase

as well as its solubility to the SEI.

To further clarify the vital impact of μ on the interphase stability, we first combined

quartz  crystal microbalance  analysis with  electrochemical methods (EQCM)  to

measure the in-situ gravimetric variation of SEI during initial Li plating and subsequent
resting processes (Figure R11).[9-11] After 300 seconds of discharge, a high mass of
13170 ng cm−2, corresponding to plated Li with SEI layer, was generated in the BM

electrolyte (Figure R11a). During the subsequent resting process, the SEI formed by
BM electrolyte slowly dissolved at a rate of 6.6 ng s−1 cm−2 due to the low dissolution

ability of BM solvent (low μ). The initial plated Li with SEI layers formed by TFPM

and TFBM electrolytes showed similar mass (Figure R11b-c). However, the SEI
dissolved rapidly in the TFBM electrolyte at a high rate of 20.9 ng s−1 cm−2 due to the
high μ of TFBM, whereas a moderate dissolution rate of 9.3 ng s−1 cm−2 was found for

the TFPM electrolyte. This balanced dissolution process facilitated the formation of a

robust inorganic-rich SEI.

Next, the sensitivity of different solvents to the electric field was evaluated through
Tafel plots (Figure R12-13).[12] Due to the higher μ, TFPM (i.e. 2.41 mA cm−2) and
TFBM (i.e. 1.26 mA cm−2) electrolytes showed higher exchange current density (j0)
than PM (i.e. 0.98 mA cm−2) and BM (i.e. 0.93 mA cm−2) electrolytes at 30 °C. However,

TFBM electrolyte provided a lower j0 than TFPM electrolyte, which may be attributed

to the high viscosity of TFBM electrolyte. Among these four electrolytes, TFPM

electrolyte exhibited the highest j0 at both 30 °C and −40 °C, which enables fast charge

transfer and rapid de-solvation, thereby achieving excellent rate and low-temperature

performance.

Based on the above thorough discussions, we can draw the conclusion that the solvent

with moderate μ could achieve a good balance between interphase stability and

sensitivity to the electric fields. The relevant data and discussions were added to the

Manuscript and Supplementary Information.

Comment #1: In the research of solvent descriptors, researchers have proposed

numbers of parameters as criteria for solvent screening, among which electrostatic

potential is also a very important screening parameter. What are the advantages of the

RESPO parameter proposed by the authors compared to electrostatic potential?

Authors’ Response: Thanks for your insightful comment, which allows us to better

clarify the advantages of the RESPO parameter. The electrostatic potential (ESP)

reflects the charge distribution on the surface of solvent molecules rather than the

intrinsic electronegativity of solvent atoms. When a strongly electronegative F atom is

close to O atom, the ESP of O atom will be significantly reduced, resulting in an ESP

value that does not match the solvent’s solubility and solvation ability, which reduces

the accuracy of ESP as a screening parameter. The restrained electrostatic potential

(RESP) is a method for calculating atomic charges and also a modified ESP method

that incorporates physical constraints (e.g., atomic electronegativity equilibration and

bond symmetry  constraints)  to  optimize  charge  distributions,  ensuring  further

=== PDF PAGE 33 ===
improvement of the rationality of the charge distribution model. RESPO method can

eliminate the interference of F atoms and accurately describe the charge distribution of

O atoms, leading to more efficient and accurate to distinguish the fluorinated ethers

with different coordination ability of O atoms. The relevant discussion has been added

to the Manuscript.

Comment #3: Among the series of fluorinated ether molecules designed, the formation

of a hexagonal coordination structure can significantly improve the performance of

batteries, especially at ultra-low temperature. What influence does this configuration

have on the lithium deposition and kinetics at low temperatures?

Authors’ Response: We thank the reviewer for this insightful comment, which allows

us to better elucidate the correlation between the six-membered chelating solvation

structure and its excellent Li compatibility at ultra-low temperatures. First, as shown in

Figure 2a-d, the six-membered ring formed by Li-TFPM complexes exhibit the most

negative binding energy, indicating a more stable structure. The synergistic O and F

chelation is anticipated to enhance the Li salt dissociation while mitigating the ionic

conductivity reduction typically associated with fluorination, thereby preventing salt

precipitation and enabling fast ion transport at ultra-low temperatures. Second, at low

temperatures, the de-solvation step becomes the rate-determining step. The stable six-

membered chelating structure with pronounced spatial effects will inhibit further

coordination of free solvents and promote anion coordination, leading to anion-rich
coordination structure of Li+(FSI−)3.03(TFPM)0.99 with an extremely high FSI−/solvent

ratio of 3.06. This consequently bestows the lowest de-solvation energy of 2.14 eV for

TFPM electrolyte, enabling uniform Li deposition with fast kinetics at low temperatures.
