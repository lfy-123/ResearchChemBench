A B S T R A C T
Phenolate–nitroaryl dyes exhibit inverted solvatochromism that is highly sensitive to the architecture of the 
𝜋-bridge. By reversing the sequence within the 𝜋-bridge of a phenolate–imine–aryl–nitroaryl framework and 
systematically varying phenylene or thienylene spacers and nitroaryl acceptors, we obtained dyes that preserve 
solvatochromic inversion behavior while shifting the solvatochromic inversion point SIP at lower solvent 
polarities with enhanced sensitivity in aprotic solvents. Multiparametric analysis shows that hydrogen bonding 
governs the negative branch of the inversion curve, whereas polarizability and dipolar interactions dominate 
the positive branch. TDDFT calculations confirm a phenolate-to-nitroaryl charge-transfer transition, modulated 
by the 𝜋-spacer. These results demonstrate that rational 𝜋-bridge engineering provides a powerful handle to 
tune both the polarity domain and the amplitude of solvatochromic inversion.
1. Introduction
Push–pull chromophores are a central class of functional organic 
sensors because of their strong sensitivity of intramolecular charge 
transfer (ICT) to subtle changes in medium polarity [1–4]. To ob-
tain strong ICT, push-pull dyes must be constructed by joining strong 
electron-donating and electron-accepting groups through 𝜋-conjugated 
bridges. This architecture confers intense visible absorption and pro-
nounced sensitivity to the surrounding medium, leading to marked sol-
vatochromic responses that are widely exploited in polarity sensing [5–
7] and optical materials [8,9].
Phenolate-based push–pull dyes incorporating nitroaryl acceptors 
are particularly notable because they often display inverted solva-
tochromism [10–13], a spectral behavior characterized by an initial 
hypsochromic shift of the ICT band with increasing solvent polarity, 
followed by a bathochromic shift from a polarity value known as a 
solvatochromic inversion point or SIP. In recent years, we have shown 
that this inversion is highly correlated with the sensitivity of the dyes 
to the polarizability, and one of the most effective ways to achieve this 
is to expand the 𝜋-bridge that connects the donor–acceptor pair of the 
dye [13–18].
In a previous study, we showed that the addition of a 1,4-phenylene 
unit in the 𝜋-bridge of the inverted solvatochromic dye 1 resulted in 
dye 2 (Fig. 1) displaying inverted solvatochromism but with a SIP

In this work, we address this question by redesigning the 𝜋-bridge

ring and three different nitroaryl acceptors were examined. By correlat-
ing the solvent-dependent spectral shifts with various solvent polarity 
scales, we show that both the spectral window and the polarity of the 
solvatochromic inversion point are modulated by the spacer position 
and identity.

The solute–solvent interactions responsible for the inverted solva-
tochromism of dyes 2–6 were quantified using Catalán’s multipara-
metric approach [19], which decomposes the effect of the solvent 
on the electronic transition energy into independent solvent contribu-
tions. Within this framework, the transition energy in solution (𝐸T) is 
expressed as: 
𝐸T = 𝐸0
T + a𝑆𝐴+ b𝑆𝐵+ c𝑆𝑃+ d𝑆𝑑𝑃
(1)
where SA represents the solvent hydrogen-bond donor acidity, SB the 
hydrogen-bond acceptor basicity, SP the solvent polarizability, and SdP
the solvent dipolarity. The regression coefficients (a-d) quantify the 
relative weight of each interaction, while their sign indicates whether a 
given solvent property contributes to a bathochromic or hypsochromic 
shift of the solvatochromic band of the dyes, allowing the dominant sta-
bilization mechanisms to be identified for each segment of the inverted 
solvatochromic curve. The complete set of coefficients, denoted a–d, is 
presented in Table S3 of the Supplementary Material.
As shown in Fig. 4, the hypsochromic shifts that define the negative 
solvatochromic branch of the curves in Fig. 3 are governed exclusively 
by SA for all dyes, demonstrating that ground-state stabilization is 
controlled by hydrogen-bond donation from the solvent. The absence 
of significant contributions from SB, SP, or SdP in this region indicates 
that neither solvent basicity nor nonspecific dipolar or dispersive in-
teractions play a relevant role in driving the negative solvatochromic 
response. In contrast, the bathochromic shifts of the solvatochromic 
absorption band, which give rise to the positive solvatochromic branch, 
display a more complex balance of solute–solvent interactions. In this 
region, the solvatochromic response arises from the combined con-
tributions of SP, SdP, and SB, with solvent polarizability (SP) acting 
as the dominant factor. The additional contributions from dipolarity 
(SdP) and hydrogen-bond acceptor basicity (SB) are of comparable 
magnitude, indicating that excited-state stabilization involves not only 
dispersive and dipolar interactions, but also specific solvent basicity 
effects. This multicomponent stabilization reflects the increased charge 
separation and polarizability of the Frank–Condon excited state of the 
dyes and accounts for the enhanced sensitivity observed in the positive 
branch of the solvatochromic curves.

more localized and hydrogen-bond-accessible ground state, in contrast 
to a more charge-separated, polarizable excited state.
The TDDFT calculations predict two low-lying electronic transitions 
with a distinct character. The higher-energy transition corresponds to a 
localized 𝜋–𝜋∗ excitation, assigned to the high-energy absorption band 
observed in the UV–Vis spectra (Fig. 3), which shows only weak solva-
tochromic sensitivity. The calculated energy for this transition (84.79 
kcal mol−1) is in reasonable agreement with the experimental value 
(77.48 kcal mol−1), supporting its assignment. In contrast, the lower-
energy transition that dominates the visible region corresponds to 
an intramolecular charge-transfer (ICT) excitation from the phenolate 
donor to the nitroaryl acceptor.
For this ICT band, the dominance of SA in the negative solva-
tochromic region reflects preferential ground-state stabilization via 
hydrogen-bond donation to the phenolate moiety, as revealed by the 
Catalán analysis. Upon excitation, electron density shifts towards the 
nitroaryl unit, reducing the relevance of hydrogen bonding and in-
creasing the sensitivity of the excited state to nonspecific solvent inter-
actions. Consequently, the positive solvatochromic region is governed 
by a cooperative contribution of SP, SdP, and SB, with polarizability 
(SP) as the leading factor, consistent with stabilization of a delocalized 
and highly polarizable ICT excited state. The close agreement between 
experimental transition energies (57.18 kcal mol−1) and calculated en-
ergies (59.44 kcal mol−1) for this band further validates the electronic 
assignment and provides a unified TDDFT–Catalán interpretation of 
inverted solvatochromic behavior.
3. Conclusions
In this work, we show how the 𝜋-bridge enables the modulation 
of inverted solvatochromism in phenolate–nitroaryl push–𝜋–pull chro-
mophores. Both the spatial arrangement of the aromatic spacer and 
the electronic characteristics of the nitroaryl acceptor exert a decisive 
influence on the solvatochromic behavior, leading to shifts in the 
solvatochromic inversion point (SIP) and alterations in solvent sensi-
tivity. Whereas the reference dye exhibits inversion only at relatively 
high solvent polarities, structural reorganization in the newly designed 
derivatives shifts the inversion towards lower polarity domains and 
produces distinct sensitivity profiles.
Multiparametric regression analysis employing Catalán’s solvent 
scales reveals a distinct mechanistic bifurcation underlying the two 
branches of the inverted solvatochromic response: negative solva-
tochromism is dictated exclusively by the hydrogen-bond donor ca-
pacity of the medium, while positive solvatochromism originates from 
a cooperative interplay between solvent polarizability, dipolarity, and 
basicity, with polarizability emerging as the predominant determinant. 
Time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT) calculations corrob-
orate that the lowest-energy absorption band arises from a phenolate-
to-nitroaryl intramolecular charge-transfer transition, yielding a highly 
polarizable excited state whose differential stabilization accounts for 
the observed solvent-dependent behavior. Collectively, these findings 
establish a unified structure–solvent–electronic correlation framework 
and identify 𝜋-bridge engineering as a powerful design strategy to 
modulate the properties of solvent-responsive chromophores.

4.3. UV/Vis measurements
UV–Vis spectra were recorded on a diode array UV–Vis near IR 
Specord S 600 (Maximum recording speed for complete spectra: 12 
msec) at a room temperature of 23 ± 1 ◦C. The molar concentration 
of the dyes in the solutions used during UV–Vis measurements was
approximately 3 ± 10−5 mol L−1.
Solvatochromic dyes 3–6 were obtained in situ by adding 5 𝜇L of a 1 
mol L−1 of an n-tetrabutylammonium hydroxide (NBu4OH) methanolic 
solution to a solution of their corresponding precursor phenols in every 
solvent before recording their spectra.
4.4. Computational calculations
All dyes were optimized at the r2SCAN-3c composite density-
functional level [21] using the RI approximation to speed up the 
calculations [22]. Frequencies were obtained at the same level to verify 
that each optimized structure was a minimum in the potential energy 
surface of the respective system. The conductor-like screening model 
was employed for modeling solvent effects, using the dielectric constant 
of dichloromethane (𝜖 = 9.08, n = 1.424). On the minima obtained, 
energies for Frank–Condon electronic transitions were calculated at 
the time-dependent density-functional level of theory (TD-DFT) with 
the 𝜔-B97X-D4 [23] and def2-TZVP [24] density functional and basis 
set, respectively, employing both the RI and the Chain of Spheres 
approximations for the Coulomb and Exchange integrals, respectively. 
The conductor polarizable continuum model (CPCM) was used to model 
solvent effects [25,26]. All TD-DFT calculations were performed with 
Orca 6.1.0 [27].
