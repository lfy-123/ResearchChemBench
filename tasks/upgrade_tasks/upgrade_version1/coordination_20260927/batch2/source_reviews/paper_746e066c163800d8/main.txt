A B S T R A C T
The novel hexa-peri-hexabenzo[7]helicene (compound 5) features a unique π-extended structure and intriguing 
photophysical properties. Elucidating the structure-property relationships is crucial for rational molecular design 
and performance optimization. Density functional theory (DFT) and time-dependent DFT (TDDFT) methods were 
employed to investigate the geometric and electronic structures, electronic absorption spectra, charge transport 
characteristics, and second-order nonlinear optical (NLO) properties of compound 5 and its π-extended de­
rivatives. Our calculations accurately reproduced the experimental absorption spectrum, allowing for the reliable 
assignment of electronic transition properties. Lateral π-extension effectively modulates the energy gap (Eg) and 
markedly enhances the second-order NLO response. Upon extending the number of benzene rings to 5n (n = 1, 2, 
3) on each lateral side of compound 13, the resulting derivatives 25, 32, and 39 exhibit a linear increase in static 
first hyperpolarizability (βHRS,∞) by a factor of 15n, while their Eg values remain nearly constant at approximately 
0.2 eV. Remarkably, compound 39 achieves the highest βHRS,∞ value of 1560 × 10−30 esu, highlighting that the 
π-extended derivatives are promising candidates for second-order NLO materials. Due to their low reorganization 
energies, the studied compounds exhibit excellent charge transport properties. We propose an optimal molecular 
design strategy enabled by precise lateral π-extension that simultaneously maximizes the transferred charge, 
confines the charge transfer distance within a target range, and preserves a favorable electron-hole overlap. This 
work provides guidelines for the design and synthesis of π-extended helicenes for high-performance 
optoelectronics.
1. Introduction
Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) are garnering increased 
attention in materials science. In particular, chiral PAHs, recognized for 
their distinctive structures and properties, are highly desirable [1–3]. 
Helicenes, a class of ortho-fused PAHs with inherent helical chirality, 
have gradually emerged as a major focus of research [4,5]. The intrinsic 
chiral π-conjugated frameworks of helicenes impart exceptional optical 
and electronic properties, enabling their application in diverse fields 
such as asymmetric catalysis [6–8], organic optoelectronics [9–13], and 
NLO materials [14–16]. Consequently, considerable efforts have been 
devoted to advancing helicene chemistry.
Conventional helicenes are called [n]helicenes, where n represents 
the number of benzene rings [17]. Studies have shown that both the 
chiral stability and chiroptical responses increase with the helical length 
n [18]. As a consequence, higher [n]helicenes have been developed 
since the [6]helicene was synthesized [19,20]. To date, the longest [n] 
helicene is [16]helicene, which was reported in 2015 [21]. However, the 
helical elongation approach suffers from limited (chiro)optical proper­
ties [21,22]. Another research direction is fusing aromatic rings to form 
π-extended helicenes [23–25]. The extension of π-conjugation can lead 
to desirable properties, such as controlling the bandgap of semi­
conductor materials that exhibit low redox potentials [26,27], and 
shifting the photophysical response into the visible and near-infrared 
regions [28].
Recently, a novel chiral homogeneous π-extended [7]helicene (hexa- 
peri-hexabenzo[7]helicene, compound 5) was reported (Fig. 1) [29]. Its 
structure consists of three perylene units fused at ortho-positions, with 
each pair of adjacent units sharing a common benzene ring [29]. The 
unique structure endows compound 5 with remarkable photophysical

Theoretical calculations serve as a powerful tool for rationalizing the 
observed properties, establishing structure-property relationships, and 
probing the photophysical properties of helicene derivatives. For 
example, Champagne and colleagues employed quantum chemical cal­
culations to elucidate the structure-NLO property relationships in 7-ring 
helicenes based on thiophene-benzene scaffolds. Their work demon­
strated that terminal nitro functionalization and thiophene-to-furan 
replacement are effective strategies for enhancing the first hyper­
polarizability [30]. A computational analysis accurately rationalized the 
absorption and emission behaviors of double aza-oxa[7]helicene, 
revealing how its structural features govern these key photophysical 
properties [31]. Liu et al. systematically investigated the effects of het­
eroatom substitution (C, Si, Ge, O, S, and Se) on the electronic structures 
and chiroptical characteristics of [7]helicene derivatives using a 
combination of DFT, TD-DFT, and the thermal vibration correlation 
function theory [32]. DFT studies have shown that structural tuning of 
the two [7]helicene subunits in π-extended double [7]helicenes enables 
precise alignment of electric and magnetic transition dipole moments, 
thereby enhancing the chiroptical response [33].
Therefore, a systematic investigation of the structure-property re­
lationships of hexa-peri-hexabenzo[7]helicene (compound 5) via quan­
tum chemical calculations is crucial to further optimize its performance 
and expand its applications. Using compound 5 as the parent molecule, 
we designed 41 π-extended derivatives by extending benzene rings at 
the top, lateral, and bottom positions to develop high-performance 
materials. In this work, we employ DFT/TDDFT calculations to inves­
tigate the geometric and electronic structures, electronic absorption and 
emission properties, charge transport characteristics, and second-order 
NLO responses of these systems. Our primary objective is to establish 
clear structure-property relationships, thereby providing theoretical 
guidance for the design and synthesis of novel high-performance 
materials.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Molecular structures
3.1.1. Molecular design
The chemical structures of the studied compounds are shown in 
Fig. 1. To investigate the evolution of the photoelectric properties from 
[7]helicene (compound 1) [57] to hexa-peri-hexabenzo[7]helicene 
(compound 5), compound 1 was selected as the reference. Compounds 
2–4 were designed by fusing benzene rings at the top 1,1′, lateral 1,1′, 
and bottom 1,1′ positions, respectively. Compound 5 is a homoge­
neously π-extended structure of compound 1. Furthermore, using com­
pound 5 as the parent molecule, derivatives 6–46 were designed 
through π-extension to explore the effects of fusing different numbers of 
benzene rings at distinct positions on the optical properties and to 
discover novel high-performance materials. Compound 6 was designed 
by fusing a benzene ring at the top 1 position of compound 5. Com­
pounds 7–9 were obtained through fusing benzene rings at the lateral 1, 
1′, 2,2′, and bottom 1,1′ positions, respectively. Benzene rings were fused 
at the top 1, lateral 1,1′, 2,2′, and bottom 1,1′ positions of compound 5 to 
form compound 10. Compounds 11–17 were designed by fusing ben­
zene rings at the top 1,1′, lateral 1,1′-5,5′, and bottom 1,1′ positions of 
compound 10, respectively. We designed compound 18 through fusing 
benzene rings at the top 1,1′, lateral 1,1′-5,5′, and bottom 1,1′ positions 
of compound 10. Compounds 19–46 are laterally π-extended derivatives 
of compound 13.
3.1.2. Geometric parameters
All ground-state geometries were optimized at the B3LYP/6-31G(d) 
level without imposing any symmetry constraints. Cartesian co­
ordinates of the optimized structures are provided in the Supplementary 
Material. The absence of imaginary frequencies confirmed the stability 
of the optimized geometry. Here, compound 5 was selected as an 
example to test the reliability of our adopted method. The calculated 
structural parameters (bond lengths and angles) obtained from the 
B3LYP functional show excellent agreement with experimental data 
(Table S1), demonstrating that the selected functional and basis set 
reliably describe molecular structures. To further strengthen our results, 
geometry optimization for compound 5 was additionally carried out at 
the dispersion-corrected DFT level using the B3LYP-D3 [58,59] func­
tional with the 6-31G(d) basis set. The key structural parameters 
calculated with the B3LYP-D3 functional show negligible deviations 
from those obtained with B3LYP, as evidenced by ≤0.007 Å for bond 
lengths and ≤0.3◦for angles (Table S1). This geometric insensitivity to 
dispersion corrections indicates that dispersion effects are not a domi­
nant factor in determining the ground-state molecular structures of the 
studied compounds.
Orthographic projections (front and side views) of the optimized 
structures for compounds 1–46 are shown in Fig. S1, with key geometric 
parameters provided in Table S2. All investigated compounds feature 
coiled helical structures, evidenced by significant overlap of terminal 
benzene rings (Fig. S1). The C–C bond lengths along the helical inner rim 
display positional symmetry in all studied compounds: C1–C2=C7–C8, 
C2–C3=C6–C7, and C3–C4=C5–C6, directly evidencing equivalent 
strain environments at symmetric sites. The mean bond lengths for 
compounds 2–4 (1.437–1.441 Å) approach the value observed in com­
pound 1 (1.445 Å). In contrast, compound 5 exhibits a significantly 
reduced mean bond length (1.429 Å, Δ = 0.016 Å), attributed to ho­
mogeneous π-extension. The mean bond lengths of designed compounds 
6–9 (1.431–1.435 Å) are comparable to that of the parent compound 5 
(1.429 Å), with a maximum deviation of 0.006 Å. Remarkably, de­
rivatives 10–46 maintain an identical mean bond length of ~1.44 Å, 
indicating that peripheral modifications exert minimal influence on the 
bond lengths of the helical core.
The geometric distortion in helical molecules is directly quantified 
by torsion angles (ϕ) along the helical inner rim. Complementing the 
bond-length symmetry, torsion angles ϕ exhibit positional equivalence 
for all compounds: ϕ1 = ϕ5 and ϕ2 = ϕ4 (Table S2). This dual-parameter 
congruence–spanning bond lengths and angular distortions–definitively 
establishes strain-field equivalence at helically symmetric sites. Com­
pound 1 exhibits a mean torsion angle of 22.3◦, whereas compounds 2–4 
display elevated values of 22.7◦–24.0◦. The calculated mean torsion 
angle of compound 5 (27.1◦) matches the experimental value (27.0◦) 
[29] within 0.1◦, further validating the computational methodology. 
Notably, this 4.8◦elevation of compound 5 over compound 1 (22.3◦) 
unambiguously demonstrates steric-driven geometric distortion at the 
helical core. The designed derivatives 6–46 exhibit reduced mean tor­
sion angles compared to the parent compound 5. Specifically, com­
pounds 19–46 maintain stable angles in a narrow range of 
approximately 23◦–24◦, demonstrating that lateral π-extension allevi­
ates steric strain at the helical core.
The centroid distance (d) between terminal benzene rings directly 
reflects the tightness of coiling in the helical structure. A smaller d in­
dicates a more tightly coiled helix. Structural analysis reveals that the 
parent compound 5 possesses a larger centroid distance (d = 4.419 Å). 
π-Extended derivatives 6–46 (excluding 8 and 9) exhibit reduced 
d values relative to the parent system, revealing that π-extension en­
hances helical tightness.
3.1.3. Planarity assessment
Molecular planarity is a key factor in characterizing the structure of 
helical π-conjugated systems. This property can be quantitatively eval­
uated using the MPP and SDP, where smaller values indicate enhanced 
planarity. The MPP, SDP, and atomic signed-distance maps relative to 
the best-fit plane for compounds 1–46 are presented in Fig. S2. As 
illustrated, the color gradient reflects the degree of deviation from the 
plane, with lighter hues indicating regions closer to the plane. Red and 
blue correspond to atomic positions above and below the fitted plane, 
respectively. The larger MPP and SDP values demonstrate low planarity 
in the studied compounds, consistent with their intrinsic helical struc­
tures. Low planarity is primarily due to atomic deviations from the plane 
in the basal region and terminal rings. Compounds 1–5 exhibit compa­
rable MPP values (~1.3 Å), indicating minimal variation in planarity. 
Compared to compound 5, the MPP values decrease for compounds 6–8 
but increase for compounds 9–18, except for compound 14. For 
example, compounds 9 and 17 exhibit the largest MPP values (1.754 Å 
and 1.824 Å, respectively). This suggests that the extended benzene 
rings at the bottom positions of compounds 5 and 10 significantly impair 
molecular planarity. The MPP values of compounds 19–46 show an 
overall progressive decrease (with minor fluctuations) compared with 
compound 13, indicating enhanced molecular planarity with an 
increasing number of laterally π-extended benzene rings. In particular, 
compounds 43–46 exhibit MPP values of approximately 0.7 Å. More­
over, when compared to the parent compound 5, a significant decrease 
in MPP values is observed for compounds 23, 24, and 26–46. Further­
more, the planarity of both left and right lateral π-extended segments

3.2. Frontier molecular orbital analysis
Frontier molecular orbital (FMO) theory is a powerful tool to ratio­
nalize the observed optoelectronic properties of various organic π-con­
jugated molecules [60]. Therefore, examining the distribution of the 
highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied 
molecular orbital (LUMO), as well as the energy gap (HOMO-LUMO 
gap), is important for the rational design and synthesis of novel mate­
rials with excellent properties. The variations in the HOMO-LUMO en­
ergy gaps and the distributions of HOMO/LUMO orbitals for the studied 
compounds are shown in Fig. 2 and Fig. S3.
Compound 1 possesses the largest energy gap (Eg = 3.87 eV) among 
the studied compounds. The energy gaps decrease sequentially from 
compound 2 (Eg = 2.35 eV) to compound 4 (Eg = 0.87 eV), demon­
strating that the fusion of benzene rings at any of the three directions 
(top, lateral, or bottom) contributes to Eg reduction. Compound 5 ex­
hibits an energy gap of 2.02 eV, a reduction of 1.85 eV compared to that 
of compound 1 (Eg = 3.87 eV), arising from homogeneous π-conjugation 
extension.
Building upon compound 5, we further extended the π-system by 
fusing additional benzene rings to develop effective strategies for energy 
gap modulation. Fusing a benzene ring at the top 1 position increased 
the Eg of compound 6 to 2.06 eV. However, the Eg decreased signifi­
cantly to 1.63 eV in compound 7 upon fusion of two benzene rings at the 
lateral 1 and 1′ positions. Fusion at the lateral 2 and 2′ positions resulted 
in compound 8, with an Eg of 2.06 eV, identical to that of compound 6. 
Compound 9, featuring fusion at the bottom 1 and 1′ positions, shows a 
slightly reduced Eg of 1.94 eV compared to compound 5. Compound 10, 
derived from compound 5 by fusing benzene rings at the top 1, lateral 
1,1′, 2,2′, and bottom 1,1′ positions, exhibits an Eg of 1.64 eV, which is 
comparable to that of compound 7. These results demonstrate that 
extension at the lateral 1 and 1′ positions of compound 5 effectively 
reduces the energy gap. A significant reduction is also achieved by 
extension at the top 1, lateral 1,1′, 2,2′, and bottom 1,1′ positions, 
yielding a similarly small energy gap. Further fusion at the top 1,1′, 
lateral 1,1′, and 5,5′ positions of compound 10 resulted in increased 
energy gaps of compounds 11, 12, and 16, respectively. In contrast, 
compounds 13, 14, 15, 17, and 18 all exhibit lower Eg values than 
compound 10. Notably, the Eg of compound 13 is significantly reduced 
to 0.49 eV.
Given the relatively narrow energy gap of compound 13 (Eg = 0.49 
eV), we strategically fused benzene rings at its lateral positions to 
construct laterally π-extended systems. Compound 19, designed by 
extending compound 13 at the lateral 1 and 1′ positions, exhibits an Eg of 
1.36 eV. When extended at the lateral 2 and 2′ positions, compound 20 
shows a lower Eg of 0.43 eV. Simultaneous fusion at both the lateral 1,1′ 
and 2,2′ positions results in compound 21 with an Eg of 1.29 eV. 
Employing the same π-extension principle used for 19–21, compounds 
22–24 exhibit energy gaps ranging from 0.34 to 0.39 eV. Notably, 
symmetrical fusion of 5 benzene rings onto each lateral side of com­
pound 13 results in a reduced Eg of 0.22 eV for compound 25. Following 
the same design strategy, compounds 32, 39, and 46 were constructed 
by symmetrically fusing 10, 15, and 20 benzene rings, respectively, onto 
each lateral side of compound 13. Compounds 32 and 39 exhibit Eg 
values (0.22 and 0.20 eV, respectively) nearly identical to that of com­
pound 25 (Eg = 0.22 eV), whereas compound 46 possesses the smallest 
Eg of 0.07 eV, which originates directly from the largest π-conjugated 
framework. Moreover, all laterally π-extended derivatives 19–46 exhibit 
substantially reduced energy gaps compared to the parent compound 5 
(Eg = 2.02 eV). The results reveal a clear trend: upon lateral extension of 
compound 13 with 5n (n = 1, 2, 3) benzene rings per side, the Eg values 
plateau at approximately 0.2 eV. However, for n = 4, the Eg value drops 
abruptly to 0.07 eV.
The FMOs of compounds 1–13, 15–17, 19–21, and 28 are delo­
calized over the entire molecular framework. In contrast, the FMOs in 
compounds 14, 18, 22–24, 26, 27, 29–31, 33, 34, 36–38, 40, 41, and 
43–45 are predominantly localized in the basal region. Another distinct 
pattern is observed for compounds 25, 32, 35, 39, and 42, where the 
FMOs are localized in both the basal and the two lateral regions. Com­
pound 46 is unique, with its FMOs confined exclusively to the lateral 
regions.
Based on the above analyses, the expansion of the π-conjugated 
system through the fusion of benzene rings not only modulates the en­
ergy gap but also alters the distribution of the frontier molecular or­
bitals. Lateral π-extension starting from compound 13 is an effective 
strategy for reducing the energy gap, as evidenced by all derivatives 
19–46 exhibiting narrower energy gaps than the parent compound 5. A 
clear trend emerges: as the structure is extended symmetrically with 5n 
(n = 1, 2, 3) benzene rings on each lateral side of compound 13, the Eg 
values remain nearly constant at approximately 0.2 eV. Such a narrow 
energy gap is anticipated to confer advantageous optical properties, 
including red-shifted absorption spectra, a strong second-order NLO 
response, and promising charge transport capabilities.

3.3.3. Emission properties
Furthermore, the emission properties of compound 5 were evaluated 
for its potential as a fluorescent probe. Based on the optimized ground 
state (S0) structure, the excited-state (S1) geometry of compound 5 was 
optimized at the TD-B3LYP/6-31G(d) level. The notable structural dif­
ference occurs in the central ortho-fused aromatic rings between the 
ground- and excited-state structures (Table S8), which can be used to 
rationalize the observed large Stokes shifts. Subsequently, the same 
method (TD-CAM-B3LYP/6-31+G(d)) as that used for electronic ab­
sorption was applied to calculate the emission wavelength. The emission 
appears at 1066 nm, corresponding to the electronic transition from 
HOMO to LUMO (Fig. 5). The dominant electronic transition is assigned 
as π→π* character within the whole molecule. Notably, the Stokes shift 
of compound 5 reaches 410 nm, indicating its potential as a fluorescent 
probe material.
3.4. Second-order NLO properties
3.4.1. Static first hyperpolarizability
The intrinsic asymmetric geometry induced by chirality, coupled 
with the pronounced intramolecular CT character, satisfies the essential 
criteria for second-order NLO materials. These findings warrant further 
investigation into their second-order NLO properties. To reliably predict 
the static first hyperpolarizability (βHRS,∞), we employed three popular 
DFT functionals (M06-2X [40,41], BHandHLYP [42,43], and 
CAM-B3LYP [44]) with the 6-31+G(d) basis set [46,47] to evaluate the 
βHRS,∞ values of nine structurally representative compounds. Notably, 
the βHRS,∞ values calculated with these three functionals exhibit 
consistent trends across the nine compounds (Table S9). It is 
well-established that the long-range-corrected functional CAM-B3LYP, 
by mitigating the electron self-interaction error, provides more accu­
rate predictions of first hyperpolarizabilities than standard functionals 
[44,68,69]. Therefore, the βHRS,∞ values of the remaining compounds 
were calculated at the CAM-B3LYP/6-31+G(d) level; the results of all 
compounds are summarized in Table 3 and Table S10. To elucidate the 
origin of the second-order NLO response, we focused on the dominant 
low-energy excited state properties (such as CT parameters) in each 
compound. This approach is consistent with the Oudar-Chemla two-le­
vel model [70,71], which simplifies the sum-over-states (SOS) frame­
work by correlating the first hyperpolarizability with low-lying charge 
transfer transitions.
The βHRS,∞ results reveal a marked second-order NLO response in the 
studied compounds, highlighting their potential as candidates for 
second-order NLO materials. For example, compound 39 exhibits the 
largest βHRS,∞ (1560 × 10−30 esu), which is greater by a factor of ~352 
than that of a bis(benzene)-substituted quinoxaline-fused [7]helicene 
(βHRS,∞ = 4.43 × 10−30 esu) [72].
Among the studied molecules, compound 1 shows the smallest βHRS,∞ 
(1.04 × 10−30 esu). The βHRS,∞ values of compounds 2 and 3 are slightly 
larger than that of compound 1. The significantly lower energy gap of 
compound 4 (0.87 eV vs. 3.87 eV for compound 1) leads to its elevated 
βHRS,∞ (8.49 × 10−30 esu). As a homogeneously π-extended derivative of 
compound 1, compound 5 possesses a marginally larger βHRS,∞ (1.92 ×
10−30 esu); this trend is consistent with its increased qCT (Table 2). 
Compounds 6 and 7 exhibit reduced βHRS,∞ values compared to com­
pound 5, whereas compounds 8–10 show increased βHRS,∞ values. The 
trend in βHRS,∞ for compounds 6–9 mirrors the parallel changes observed 
in their dCT, ΔμGS−ES, and t values (see Section 3.3.2). Despite its smaller 
dCT, ΔμGS−ES, and t values, compound 10 exhibits a larger ground-state 
dipole moment than compound 5 (0.90 D vs. 0.61 D), which accounts 
for the enhanced βHRS,∞. The βHRS,∞ values of compounds 11–18 are all 
higher than that of compound 10, with compound 15 being the only 
exception. The lower βHRS,∞ value of 15 is attributed to its strongest 
electron-hole overlap (t = −3.161 Å) among these compounds. Inter­
estingly, the βHRS,∞ of compound 13 (34.38 × 10−30 esu) is ~13 times 
higher than that of compound 10, due to its improved charge transfer 
characteristics, as reflected in its superior CT metrics.
Shifting focus to the laterally π-extended derivatives 19–46, com­
pounds 19 and 21 exhibit distinctly smaller βHRS,∞ values (6.20 and 7.04 
× 10−30 esu, respectively) than compound 13. In contrast, compounds 
20 and 22–46 show βHRS,∞ values ranging from 22.07 to 1560 × 10−30 
esu. This broad range of βHRS,∞ highlights the profound influence of 
lateral π-extension on the static first hyperpolarizability, with most de­
rivatives demonstrating significantly enhanced performance relative to 
13. Moreover, derivatives 19–46 all exhibit a marked enhancement in 
βHRS,∞ compared to the parent compound 5.
Remarkably, compounds 25, 32, and 39–designed by laterally 
extending the structure of compound 13 with 5, 10, and 15 benzene 
rings per side, respectively–maintain energy gaps at ~0.2 eV. These 
compounds exhibit significantly enhanced βHRS,∞ values of 496.7 ×
10−30, 1076 × 10−30, and 1560 × 10−30 esu–representing approxi­
mately 15-, 31-, and 45-fold increases relative to compound 13. The 
markedly improved second-order NLO responses arise not only from 
extended π-conjugation but, more critically, from an optimized CT 
character. EDDM analyses reveal that compounds 25, 32, and 39 exhibit 
efficient multidirectional CT–a distinct contrast to the unidirectional

3.4.2. Analysis of DR, ρ, and dominant NLO contributions
The depolarization ratio (DR) and nonlinear anisotropy parameter 
(ρ) are critical for characterizing NLO materials, as they quantify the 
relative contributions of the dipolar (|βJ=1|) and octupolar (|βJ=3|) 
tensorial components to the first hyperpolarizability tensor. The calcu­
lated parameters–including DR, ρ, the |βJ=1| and |βJ=3| components, 
and the dipolar (Φ(βJ=1)) and octupolar (Φ(βJ=3)) contribution 
ratios–are summarized in Table 3 and Table S10.
DR > 4.26 with ρ < 1 indicates that the second-order NLO response is 
dipolar-dominated, whereas DR < 4.26 with ρ > 1 suggests octupolar 
dominance [55]. The results demonstrate that the octupolar component 
(|βJ=3|) dominates over its dipolar counterpart (|βJ=1|) in most com­
pounds. For example, the second-order NLO responses of compounds 3, 
4, 6, 7, 10, 13, 14, 18–24, and 26–46 arise primarily from octupolar 
contributions, because of their low depolarization ratios (DR < 4.26) 
and high nonlinear anisotropy parameters (ρ > 1). Notably, compounds 
3, 7, and 21 exhibit very low DR values of ~1.5, consistent with a 
predominantly octupolar character, a conclusion further solidified by 
their high Φ(βJ=3) contribution ratios (0.97, 0.95, and 0.92, respec­
tively). Conversely, dipolar components dominate the second-order NLO 
responses of the remaining compounds (1, 2, 5, 8, 9, 11, 12, 15–17, and 
25), as evidenced by DR > 4.26 and ρ < 1.

3.5. Reorganization energy
The total reorganization energy (λ) comprises both internal (λint) and 
external (λext) reorganization energies. The λext arises from polarization 
of the surrounding medium, which is generally very small and can be 
neglected in nonpolar solvents [74,75]. Consequently, only the λint was 
considered in this study. The λint is a critical parameter that governs 
charge carrier (electron and hole) mobilities in organic molecular sys­
tems. It is defined as the sum of geometric relaxation energies during the 
interconversion between ionic (i.e., anion for electron and cation for 
hole) and neutral states [76]. The adiabatic potential energy surface 
approach was employed to calculate hole and electron reorganization 
energies (λh, λe) for structurally representative compounds, as illustrated 
in Fig. 8. The calculated reorganization energies at the B3LYP/6-31G(d) 
level are summarized in Table 4.
The λh and λe values of compound 1 were calculated to be 0.213 and 
0.244 eV, respectively. In contrast, compounds 5, 10, 13, 18, 25, 32, and 
39 exhibit significantly lower reorganization energies. This reduction 
facilitates more efficient charge transport, a finding further corrobo­
rated by their CT parameters (Table 2). For example, compounds 5, 10, 
13, 25, 32, and 39 display larger transferred charge (qCT = 0.385–0.620 
e−) compared to compound 1 (qCT = 0.316 e−). Notably, compounds 13, 
25, 32, and 39 stand out, demonstrating concurrently elevated qCT, dCT, 
and ΔμGS−ES values. This synergistic enhancement of key CT descriptors 
underpins their superior charge transfer efficiency, which is directly 
reflected in their reorganization energies being among the lowest within 
the series. These results establish the critical role of expanded π-conju­
gation in enhancing charge carrier mobility. Among these compounds, 
compound 46 exhibits the highest λh and λe values, corresponding to its 
relatively compromised charge transport efficiency–as further evi­
denced by its minimal qCT, dCT, and ΔμGS−ES parameters. This reaffirms 
that an extension up to 15 benzene rings on each lateral side of com­
pound 13 represents the performance optimum; beyond this size, key 
functional properties begin to degrade.
The λh values of these compounds are smaller than the λe values 
except for compounds 13 and 39, indicating that hole transport is 
favored over electron transport. Interestingly, the reorganization energy 
decreases linearly with the energy gap. For instance, for compounds 1, 
5, 10, 13, and 18, the Eg values follow the order Eg (1) > Eg (5) > Eg (10) 
> Eg (18) > Eg (13), and the λh and λe values exhibit the same trend: λ 
(1) > λ (5) > λ (10) > λ (18) > λ (13). The hole reorganization energies 
(λh) of the investigated compounds 1, 5, 10, 13, 18, 25, 32, 39, and 46 
range from 0.019 to 0.269 eV. These λh values are smaller than that of N, 
N′-diphenyl-N,N′-bis(3-methylphenyl)-([1,1′-biphenyl])-4,4′-diamine 
(TPD), a typical hole transport material with λh = 0.290 eV [77]. The 
electron reorganization energies (λe = 0.019–0.244 eV) of compounds 1, 
5, 10, 13, 18, 25, 32, and 39 are lower than the value of 0.276 eV re­

for 
the 
excellent

tron reorganization energy (λe = 0.295 eV) of compound 46 is slightly 
higher than that of Alq. Lower reorganization energy is associated with 
high charge carrier mobility [79], demonstrating that the studied 
compounds exhibit excellent charge transport properties.
4. Conclusions
In this study, we systematically investigated the geometric and 
electronic structures, electronic absorption spectra, charge transport 
characteristics, and second-order NLO properties of hexa-peri-hex­
abenzo[7]helicene and its π-extended derivatives using DFT/TDDFT 
methods to gain a comprehensive understanding of their structure- 
property relationships and explore potential applications. The simu­
lated electronic absorption spectrum closely matches its experimental 
counterpart, enabling confident assignment of electronic transition 
properties. Lateral π-extension not only effectively tunes the energy gap 
but also significantly enhances the second-order NLO response. When
