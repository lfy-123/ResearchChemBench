A B S T R A C T
Two novel sulfonamide derivatives, 4-amino-N-(naphthalen-2-yl)benzenesulfonamide (SNaft) and 4-amino-N- 
(anthracen-2-yl)benzenesulfonamide (SAntr), were synthesized and comprehensively characterized using spec­
troscopic, crystallographic, chromatographic, and computational methods. Spectrophotometric titration 
confirmed two acid–base equilibria associated with amino and amide proton dissociation. Chromatographic 
analysis indicated moderate lipophilicity and strong affinity toward phospholipids and human serum albumin. 
Single-crystal X-ray diffraction revealed that SNaft crystallizes in the orthorhombic Pbca space group, while 
SAntr adopts a monoclinic P2₁/c symmetry. Both compounds exhibit typical sulfonamide geometries, charac­
terized by partial sp² hybridization at the sulfonamide nitrogen, which forms supramolecular frameworks sta­
bilized by N–H⋯O/N hydrogen bonds, π–π stacking, and C–H⋯π interactions. DFT calculations supported the 
experimental findings, revealing V- and Z-shaped conformational minima stabilized by intramolecular hydrogen 
bonding. Biological assays demonstrated that SNaft exhibits more selective anticancer and antifungal activity, 
whereas SAntr remained inactive. The integrated structural, spectroscopic, and bioanalytical approach provides 
valuable insights into structure–property–bioactivity relationships within aromatic sulfonamide frameworks.
1. Introduction
The widespread use of antibiotics (and other therapeutic agents) for 
treating common diseases has led to a significant increase in drug 
resistance. This involves the ability of pathogenic microorganisms to 
develop various mechanisms that enable them to survive in the presence 
of the drug's active ingredient. A similar phenomenon is also observed in 
cancers [1,2]. This increasingly severe emergence of drug resistance 
results in enormous health care expenditure, difficulties in selecting 
appropriate therapies, and, consequently, prolonged recovery times. 
How can this progressive problem of multidrug resistance be tackled 
effectively? There are two main approaches: identifying compounds 
with promising application potential that can be further optimized, and 
discovering new molecular targets for known compounds (i.e., redi­
recting them toward a previously unexplored interaction with a 
biomolecule). The design of analogues of widely used pharmacological 
agents focuses on increasing their biological efficacy or broadening their 
spectrum of action. In some cases, the modification or introduction of a 
single substituent can completely change the pharmacological profile of 
a compound. A representative example is fabimycin, where introducing 
an amino group has improved efficacy against more than 200 strains of 
microorganisms resistant to commercially used drugs [3]. Combating 
drug resistance can also occur at the molecular level, such as developing 
peptide-based antimicrobial agents, isolating peptides, or searching for 
entirely new preparations. Peptides and their mimetics have recently 
garnered attention as promising candidates to address the issue of 
antibiotic resistance. They exhibit broad-spectrum activity, high speci­
ficity, and a lower propensity for resistance development compared to 
conventional antibiotics. However, these compounds also possess 
inherent limitations, including poor stability under physiological

limited oral bioavailability, and difficulties in large-scale synthesis and 
formulation [4,5]. Depending on the specific molecular structure and 
functional groups, the resulting derivative's biological activity can 
manifest in different ways – by inhibiting biochemical pathways,

During the 20th century, benzenesulfonic acid amides (sulfon­
amides) were widely used therapeutics in human and veterinary medi­
cine. Owing to their structural similarity to p-aminobenzoic acid 
(PABA), sulfonamides act as competitive inhibitors of the bacterial 
enzyme dihydropteroate synthetase, an enzyme involved in folic acid 
biosynthesis essential for bacterial growth and nucleic acid synthesis. 
Sulfonamides inhibit the activity of this enzyme, which catalyzes the 
condensation of PABA and 7,8-dihydro-6-hydroxy-methylpterine pyro­
phosphate to form the intermediate dihydropteroate, a crucial step in 
folic acid biosynthesis, ultimately leading to the formation of dihy­
drofolic acid [13]. In addition, sulfonamides inhibit zinc-containing 
carbonic anhydrases, enzymes crucial for acid–base homeostasis and 
carbon dioxide transport. Because most physiologically important car­
bonic anhydrase isozymes are located intracellularly, their inhibitors 
must demonstrate the ability to cross biological barriers to reach the 
active sites of target enzymes. Thus, the lipophilicity of sulfonamide 
derivatives must be considered one of the critical parameters [14–17]. 
Moreover, suitably modified sulfonamides can inhibit topoisomerases, 
the enzymes that play a key role in DNA replication and repair. They are 
responsible for cleaving and rejoining the DNA helix. Topoisomerase I 
cleaves a single strand of nucleic acid, while topoisomerase II cleaves 
both strands. Both enzymes are of considerable interest for research as 
possible targets in cancer therapy [18–21].
Beyond their role as enzyme inhibitors, it is also essential to consider 
the interactions of sulfonamides with other vital biomolecules and their 
potential effects on the human body. In previous work, we have shown 
using computational methods that sulfonamides with alkyl fragments 
and sulfathiazole can bind to the minor groove of DNA [1,7]. Further­
more, we demonstrated that complexing sulfonamides with metal ions 
increases their biological activity compared to ligands alone [22]. It is 
also crucial to consider the demonstrated allergenic effects of some 
sulfonamides on the human body, which underscores the need for 
designing novel compounds with low allergenic potential [23,24]. 
Human serum albumin (HSA) is the most abundant protein in human 
plasma and plays a central role in the transport of endogenous and 
exogenous compounds. Due to its well-defined structure and multiple 
ligand-binding sites, HSA is widely used as a model protein in studies of 
drug–protein interactions and may also represent an important molec­
ular target influencing the distribution and biological activity of bioac­
tive compounds. Evaluation of compound binding to HSA is possible 
using,

Immobilized-HSA chromatographic assay has been proposed as a rapid, 
experimentally grounded screening approach to estimate HSA affinity in 
early ADME profiling. The high affinity for plasma proteins can raise 
several safety concerns, such as reduced clearance, limited brain pene­
tration, and diminished pharmacological efficacy, since only unbound 
molecules can interact with their intended targets [25–28].
This study aims to elucidate how structural modifications in aro­
matic sulfonamides affect their spectroscopic, physicochemical, and 
biological properties through an integrated experimental and compu­
tational approach. The primary objective of this study was to synthesize 
and comprehensively characterize two novel sulfonamide derivatives, 4- 
amino-N-(naphthalen-2-yl)benzenesulfonamide (SNaft) and 4-amino-N- 
(anthracen-2-yl)benzenesulfonamide (SAntr), to explore how structural 
modifications within aromatic systems influence their physicochemical 
and biological properties. By integrating spectroscopic, crystallo­
graphic, and computational analyses with preliminary bioassays, this 
work aims to elucidate the relationship between molecular structure, 
supramolecular organization, and selective bioactivity, providing a 
foundation for the rational design of low-toxicity sulfonamide-based 
agents [29–32].

2.4. Crystal structure determination
Single-crystal X-ray diffraction data for SNaft were collected at 120.0 
(2) K on an IPDS 2T dual-beam diffractometer equipped with a GeniX 3D 
Mo HighFlux microfocus X-ray source (Mo Kα radiation, λ = 0.71069 Å, 
50 kV, 1.0 mA). The crystal was maintained at 120 K under a nitrogen 
stream using a CryoStream-800 device throughout the experiment. Data 
collection and reduction were performed with the X-Area 1.75 program 
suite [34].
Single-crystal X-ray diffraction data for SAntr were collected at 120.0 
(1) K on a Rigaku Oxford Diffraction SuperNova dual-source diffrac­
tometer equipped with a Cu Kα micro-focus X-ray source (λ = 1.54184 
Å) and a HyPix detector [35]. Data collection, cell refinement, and 
reduction were done using CrysAlisPro 1.171.44.111a (Rigaku Oxford 
Diffraction, 2025). An empirical absorption correction based on spher­
ical harmonics, implemented in the SCALE3 ABSPACK algorithm, was 
applied [36].
The structures were solved by intrinsic phasing using SHELXT [37] 
and refined with Olex2 [38] and SHELXL-2015 [39]. Figures were pre­
pared with Diamond [40]. All non-hydrogen atoms were refined 
anisotropically. Hydrogen atoms were placed in idealized positions and 
refined using the riding model with appropriate constraints. Crystallo­
graphic data for the reported structures have been deposited with the 
Cambridge Crystallographic Data Centre under deposition numbers 
CCDC 2483756 and 2489876. These data can be obtained free of charge 
from the CCDC via www.ccdc.cam.ac.uk/structures.

pH-spectrophotometric titration. UV-Vis spectra were measured in the

2.7. Lipophilicity, drug-plasma proteins binding, and phospholipid affinity 
assays
All chromatographic experiments were performed according to the 
procedures developed by Valko and co-workers [44,45] and imple­
mented in our laboratory [46–48]. Biomimetic chromatography offers 
the experimental determination of lipophilicity and affinity to phos­
pholipids, expressed as Chromatographic Hydrophobicity Index (CHI) 
values. It also enables the assessment of plasma proteins through a 
gradient elution experiment and comparison with model substances.
The Prominence-1 LC-2030C 3D HPLC system, managed by LabSo­
lution software, was employed for all chromatographic analyses. 
Throughout the study, three different types of chromatographic columns 
were utilized: 
• IAM.PC.DD2 (10 × 4.6 mm; 10.0 µm with a guard column; Regis 
Technologies; USA);
• Gemini NX- C18 (50 × 3.0 mm; 5.0 µm, Phenomenex, USA);
• Chiralpak® HSA (50 × 4 mm; 5 µm with safety guard column; Daicel 
Chiral Technologies, USA).
Artificial Membrane Chromatography (IAM) and human serum al­
bumin (HSA) affinity measurements were carried out using mobile 
phase A, which consisted of a 50 mM ammonium acetate solution 
adjusted to physiological pH 7.4 with concentrated ammonia solution. 
CHI logD values were determined in three pH conditions: 50 mM 
ammonium acetate adjusted to pH 7.4 and 10.5, and acetic acid adjusted 
to 2.6. The mobile phase was prepared using ultrapure water obtained 
from a Milli-Q water purification system, which had a resistivity of 18.2 
MΩ.
Phospholipid binding measurements employed mobile phase B, 
consisting of HPLC-grade acetonitrile using a linear gradient from 0 to 
85 % B over 5.25 min, followed by a 0.5 min hold at 85 % ACN. The 
mobile phase was pumped at a flow rate of 1.5 mL/min, and the IAM.PC. 
DD2 column was maintained at 30 ◦C.
The same solvents as those used in IAM chromatography were 
applied for lipophilicity measurements. The flow rate was set to 0.9 mL/ 
min, and the column temperature was maintained at 25 ◦C. A linear 
gradient from 0 to 100 % ACN was applied over 0–3.5 min, with the 
maximum phase B concentration maintained for 1 min, followed by a 2 
min equilibration before the subsequent analysis.
Determination of %HSA binding was performed using HPLC-grade 
isopropanol as mobile phase B. A linear gradient from 0 % to 30 % 
isopropanol was applied over 5 minutes, followed by an isocratic hold at 
30 % for 4 minutes. During the final 5 min of the analysis, the mobile 
phase mixture was replaced with pure ammonium acetate solution. The 
column temperature was kept at 30 ◦C, and the flow rate was set to 0.9 
mL/min.

2.8. Computational study
The relaxed scans along C–S–N–C torsional angle [33,50,51] were 
performed for SNaft and SAntr using the Regularized variant of the 
Strongly Constrained and Appropriately Normed semilocal density 
functional – R2SCAN [52,53]. The triple-ζ valence plus polarization 
basis set from the Ahlrichs def2 family (def2-TZVP) [54] and a matching 
Coulomb-fitting auxiliary basis set (def2/J) [55] were used. The 
two-electron Coulomb integrals were calculated using the split-RI-J 
approximation [56]. The presence of the solvent (methanol, water, 
n-octanol) was simulated using the Conductor-like Polarizable Contin­
uum Model (C-PCM) solvation [57]. Structures corresponding to the 
minima were then optimized at the same level of theory. Vibrational 
analyses were performed following all optimizations to ensure that the 
found stationary points are true minima on the potential energy surface. 
The wavefunction files used for further electron density topology ana­
lyses were obtained using ωB97M-V range-separated functional [58] and 
methanol as a solvent. All of the remaining settings were identical to 
those used above. The ωB97M-V functional is one of the most accurate 
DFT methods available for various properties [59–61]. The dipole mo­
ments presented in this paper are based on the ωB97M-V wavefunction. 
All DFT calculations were carried out using ORCA (version 6.0.1) [62].

2.9. Biological tests (antifungal, antimicrobial, and anticancer activity)
Human cancer cell lines, including A-549 (non-small cell lung car­
cinoma, CCL-185), MCF-7 (breast adenocarcinoma, HB-8065), Nalm-6 
(lymphoblastic leukemia, CVCL-0092), HCT-116 (colorectal carcinoma, 
CVCL-427), and the non-cancerous HEK293 cell line (human embryonic 
kidney), were maintained under standard conditions. A-549, MCF-7, 
and Nalm-6 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium, while HCT- 
116 cells were grown in McCoy’s 5A medium and HEK293 cells in 
Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM). All culture media were 
supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS), 2 mM L-glutamine, 
and antibiotics: 62.6 μg/mL penicillin and 40 μg/mL streptomycin. Cells 
were incubated at 37 ◦C in a humidified atmosphere with 5 % CO₂. 
Regular testing for Mycoplasma contamination was performed.
Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay (3-(4,5-dimethylth­
iazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide). Cells were seeded into 
96-well plates and treated with the tested compounds at concentrations 
ranging from 0 to 100 μM for 72 hours. Cisplatin was included as a

3. Results and discussion
Single-crystal X-ray diffraction revealed that compound SNaft 
(Fig. 4) crystallizes in the orthorhombic space group Pbca, with eight 
molecules in the unit cell. The aromatic core is essentially planar, and 
the benzene ring's C–C bond lengths [1.37–1.42 Å] are characteristic of 
a delocalized π system [33]. The sulfonamide fragment displays typical 
geometry: S––O bond lengths of 1.4395(14) and 1.4398(13) Å, S–N =
1.6288(15) Å, and S–C = 1.7569(18) Å, all within the ranges observed 
for aryl sulfonamides [33,69,70]. Angles around the sulfur atom 
[∠O–S–O = 118.5◦, ∠N–S–C = 107.6◦] confirm a distorted tetra­
hedral environment. The geometry at the sulfonamide nitrogen is 
particularly noteworthy, with ∠C–N–S = 125.1◦, indicative of partial 
sp² hybridization and consistent with conjugation of the nitrogen lone 
pair with the antibonding orbitals of the S––O group [33,69,70]. 
Torsional angles about the S–N bond adopt characteristic anti/gauche 
conformations [τ(C1–N1–S1–O1) = 171.6◦(anti), τ(C1–N1–S1–O2) =
–61.1◦(gauche)], a motif commonly observed in sulfonamides [33,70].
In the crystal lattice, directional N–H⋯O hydrogen bonds (Table 1) 
dominate, forming catemeric chains propagating along the b axis [71]. 
Graph-set analysis confirms the C(n) motifs typical of sulfonamides [33,
71,72]. Proton N2–H2A engages in bifurcated interactions with two 
symmetry-related O1 acceptors: N2–H2A⋯O1(1–x, –½ +
y ); 
N2–H2A⋯O1(3/2–x, –½+y, z), while N2–H2B forms a more directional 
contact to O2(½–x, –½ + y, z). An additional N1–H1⋯N2(3/2–x, ½ + y, 
z) hydrogen bond cross-links neighboring chains. Two intramolecular 
C–H⋯O interactions C2–H2⋯O2 and C16–H16⋯O2 preorganize the 
sulfonamide fragment and may direct the hydrogen-bonding pattern 
[33,71]. π–π stacking further stabilizes the packing. The shortest 
centroid–centroid distance is 3.617 Å with negligible twist (α ≈0.0◦), 
moderate offsets (β = γ ≈30.6◦), and a slippage of ~1.96 Å, consistent 
with efficient parallel π–π overlap [33,73,74]. Additional C–H⋯π 
contacts are present [H⋯Cg = 2.70–2.98 Å, ∠X–H⋯Cg = 114–135◦] 
[73,74]. Collectively, the directional N–H⋯O/N hydrogen bonds, 
reinforced by π–π stacking and C–H⋯π interactions, generate a compact 
three-dimensional supramolecular network devoid of solvent-accessible 
voids.
Compound SAntr crystallizes in the monoclinic space group P2₁/c 
with four molecules per unit cell. The aromatic fragments are planar, 
with mean C–C bond lengths of ~1.40 Å, consistent with π-electron 
delocalization [33]. The geometry of the sulfonamide fragment closely 
parallels that observed in SAntr, with S––O bond lengths of 1.430(1) and 
1.437(1) Å, S–N = 1.622(1) Å, and S–C = 1.758(1) Å. The O–S–N, 
O–S–C, and N–S–C bond angles (103.6◦, 109.6◦, and 107.8◦) confirm 
a distorted tetrahedral arrangement, while the widened C–N–S angle 
(125◦) again indicates partial sp² character of the sulfonamide nitrogen 
[33,69,70].
As in SNaft, N–H⋯O hydrogen bonds dominate the crystal packing, 
generating catemeric chains. However, in SAntr, these chains propagate 
along the c-axis rather than the b-axis. They are further cross-linked by an 
N–H⋯N contact, reinforcing the supramolecular framework. Intra­
molecular C–H⋯O interactions, analogous to those in SNaft, stabilize 
the sulfonamide fragment and direct the hydrogen-bonding pattern [33,
70–72]. Thus, the hydrogen-bonding motifs in compound SAntr strongly 
resemble those observed in SNaft, differing mainly in crystallographic 
symmetry and the propagation direction of the chains. π–π stacking in­
teractions also contribute to lattice stabilization in SAntr. As in SNaft, 
they stabilize the aromatic framework, but with slightly different 
geometrical features. The shortest centroid–centroid distance is 3.75 Å, 
longer than the 3.617 Å observed in SNaft, and accompanied by modest

The biological activity of chemical compounds is closely related to 
their specific proteolytic, complex, solvation or conformational form 
[76,77]. Both the route of drug absorption as well as its interaction with 
the relevant receptor or other structural element of the body are criti­
cally important [76,78]. All these factors are closely interrelated, mak­
ing them essential to define and understand them in detail.
By studying the acid-base characteristics of sulfonamide derivatives, 
the predominant form of the molecule at a given pH can be determined, 
which facilitates the assessment of their potential as therapeutic sub­
stances [76]. For sulfonamides, the protolytic form is the primary 
determinant of their action, as their antimicrobial activity depends on 
their ability to exist in a non-dissociated form that allows them to 
penetrate bacterial cell membranes [76]. The penetration of these 
compounds into tissues requires lipid solubility, so the non-dissociated 
form is crucial during the transport phase. In contrast, in interacting 
with the receptor inside the bacterial cell, the anionic form is the active 
form [77]. Given these relationships, it is important to determine the 
dissociation constants of the two functional groups capable of ionization 
in the sulfonamide molecule. The derivatives tested showed poor solu­
bility in water, so solutions in methanol were used to determine the pKa 
values. Spectrophotometric titration was used as the measurement 
method. In a first step, UV-Vis spectra of the compounds were recorded 
in acidic (methanesulfonic acid) and basic (tetrabutylammonium hy­
droxide) solution. Spectral effects were observed in all measurements: 
for SNaft in acidic solution, there was a hypochromic effect, while in 
basic solution, there was also a hypochromic effect combined with a 
bathochromic shift. A hypochromic effect was evident for SAntr in both 
acidic and basic media. The observed spectral changes confirmed that 
the chosen method allows reliable determination of the values of the 
dissociation constants. The stability of the ionized forms was confirmed 
by the absence of changes in the recorded spectra over time. Titrations 
were carried out under conditions allowing the observation of absor­
bance changes at appropriate pH values, which made it possible to 
accurately determine the acid-base characteristics of the compounds 
studied. Fig. 3. shows the assumed dissociation model for the derivatives 
under study, where two equilibria related to proton dissociation can be

Regarding the equilibrium structures of the studied compounds, the 
CSNC dihedral angle (as defined in Fig. 7) scans suggest the existence of 
three minima (Fig. 8) for both SNaft and SAntr regardless of ionization 
state. Geometrically, two of these minima adopt a V-like conformation 
with CSNC dihedral angle value close to ±50 degrees [33,70]. The 
remaining minimum is Z-Shaped with a value of the corresponding 
dihedral angle at about 180 degrees. Analysis of the chart reveals that 
the V-like structures are nearly equivalent in energy, whereas the Z 
conformer is energetically less favorable. The neutral systems' differ­
ences are most pronounced, amounting to 3.05 and 3.03 kcal/mol for 
SNaft and SAntr, respectively. These differences mainly originate from 
intramolecular hydrogen bonds in the V-shaped conformers, which are 
discussed in detail in the following sections of this manuscript.

Polarity is of utmost importance when describing potential drugs, as 
it allows one to assess the dissolution processes in gastrointestinal fluids 
and passing through cell membranes. Thus, in this paper, polarity was 
examined from multiple perspectives by assessing: dipole moment, polar 
surface area, logP, and logD values (vide Table 4). All parameters used to 
evaluate the tested systems' polarity level indicate a significant value of 
this quantity. Initially, it seems quite unexpected that sulfonamides 
substituted with non-polar systems such as naphthalene or anthracene 
are significantly polar, especially regarding V-shaped systems. As shown 
in Fig. S13 using SNaft-V(+)as an example, the dipole moment vector is 
located in a way that makes the sulfonamide group the negative end of 
the vector (vertex of the V), whereas the aromatic rings of both PAH and 
sulfonanilide part constitute a positive end of the dipole moment vector 
(arms of the V). Analogous sulfanilamide derivatives based on thiazolo 
[4,5-d]pyrimidine moiety investigated as potential Carbonic Anhy­
drase II (CA II) and Dickkopf-1 (DKK1) inhibitors were found to have a 
dipole moment higher than 13D in most cases, regardless of the basis set 
used during the calculations [82].
Similar to the dipole moment, polar surface values also reflect sig­
nificant polarity of studied systems, as for all studied compounds, it was 
calculated to account for 67-69 % of the total surface area. This trans­
lates to a polar surface area above 210 Å2 for both studied systems in 
each conformation. As such, they fail to obey Veber’s rule, which states 
that only compounds with a polar surface area equal to or less than 140 
Å2 are likely to have significant oral bioavailability [83]. In like manner, 
log P values calculated for each studied conformer are negative, indi­
cating hydrophilicity. With the value of -0.632, SAntr-Z is the least hy­
drophilic outlier here. The values of logP calculated for the remaining 
conformers fall into the -1.200 to -1.500 range. The lipophilicity order 
may thus look as follows: SAntr-V(+) ≈SNaft-V(–) ≈SNaft-Z < SNaft-V 
(+) < SAntr-V(–) < SAntr-Z. Two reasons exist for the differences be­
tween logP values determined experimentally and by computational 
methods. First, naphthalene and anthracene moieties may favor π–π 
stacking while in solution, creating corresponding dimers or even olig­
omers. This can significantly increase partitioning, making the experi­
mental values more lipophilic than the monomer-based calculations. 
Second, the calculations are based on a single structure within a solvent 
approximated by a continuum, thus missing possible conformers and 
protomer populations present during experimental measurements.

conformers of SNaft and SAntr are collected in Table S7. The values of

oxygen inside the molecule, the discussed H-bond effectively lowers the 
resulting polarity, thereby increasing passive membrane permeability. 
This effect is mirrored in the polarity assessments.

Antifungal evaluation showed that SNaft exhibited selective activity

The biological evaluation highlights SNaft as the more promising of 
the two synthesized compounds, displaying moderate and selective 
anticancer activity and limited antifungal potency against Candida 
albicans SC5314. In contrast, SAntr remained inactive across all tested 
models. Neither compound demonstrated antibacterial activity, under­
scoring their narrow biological spectrum compared with clinically used 
reference drugs. Although the potency of SNaft was considerably lower 
than that of cisplatin, levofloxacin, or fluconazole, its selective activity 
against cancer cells and lack of cytotoxicity toward nonmalignant HEK- 
293 cells suggest potential for further structural optimization and 
development.
4. Conclusions
Comprehensive spectroscopic, crystallographic, and computational 
analyses of the novel sulfonamides SNaft and SAntr revealed comparable 
local geometries but distinct supramolecular organizations governed by 
crystal symmetry. Both derivatives exhibit amphoteric acid–base 
behavior, low polarity, and moderate lipophilicity, as confirmed by 
spectrophotometric and chromatographic studies. DFT calculations 
revealed the predominance of V-shaped conformers stabilized by 
intramolecular hydrogen bonding, which is consistent with their 
experimental characteristics.
Biological evaluation demonstrated that SNaft exhibits selective 
anticancer and antifungal activity, whereas SAntr remains inactive, 
underscoring the influence of subtle structural and conformational dif­
ferences on bioactivity. The observed variations in biological response 
likely arise from differences in supramolecular packing and molecular 
polarity, which affect solubility and biomolecular interactions.
Although both compounds show strong binding to serum albumin, 
suggesting limited bioavailability, their structural features make them 
attractive candidates for further modification or metal coordination. The 
present findings provide valuable insight into the structur­
e–property–bioactivity relationships of aromatic sulfonamides and 
establish a foundation for the design of new bioactive and coordination- 
capable derivatives.
The conducted research significantly advances knowledge of 
structure-property-activity relationships in the sulfonamide group of 
compounds, offering new insights into the possibilities for their rational 
design. The proposed research approach is distinguished by its 
comprehensiveness and may provide a valuable foundation for further 
work on safer and more selective bioactive compounds.
