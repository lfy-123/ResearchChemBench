ABSTRACT: A family of 9-substituted fluorene derivatives has been
synthesized, which are potent super-reductants upon photoexcitation at
their anionic state. Electronic tunability and consequent modulation of the
ground-state redox behavior can be easily achieved for this set of molecules.
The entire class of fluorene anions is extremely easy to oxidize at their
ground state, showing oxidation in the range of −0.69 to −0.36 V vs SCE,
which also makes them super-reductants (Eox* = circa −3 V), once
photoexcited. Moreover, these anions are very long-lived in their excited
state and display delayed fluorescence. One representative molecule, 9-Ph-
fluorene, exhibits a singlet excited-state lifetime of 43.3 ns, while the delayed component is long-lived at 19.2 μs. The molecule also
reaches its triplet state as evident from the measured phosphorescence at 77 K. This molecule homolytically cleaves the reductively
recalcitrant C(sp2)−Cl bond, generating an aryl radical at RT under white light excitation. The super-reductive power of the class of
photocatalysts has been utilized for a set of important chemical transformations, including hydrodehalogenation, C−C cross-
coupling, and borylation, from aryl chloride substrates, offering great promise for their use as photocatalysts.
KEYWORDS: fluorene, excited state, super-reductant, triplet state, single electron transfer
■INTRODUCTION
Recent developments in small-molecule photocatalysis have
generated significant momentum in synthetic organic trans-
formations, which are otherwise very challenging via a thermal
pathway.1−3 The visible-light-absorbing property of a molecule
provides a unique advantage to the molecule since the
absorbed photon energy remains stored in the molecule.
This excess energy significantly tunes the excited state of the
molecule, thereby considerably enhancing both the oxidizing
and reducing powers of the molecule.4−6 Utilizing such
strategies, several organic transformations have been con-
ducted that were essentially impossible to perform under
thermal reaction conditions.7−10
Many prior studies are dedicated to discover new strategies
to make molecules with impressive excited-state redox
properties.11−14 Absorption of two consecutive photons
(Con-PET) by a molecule has been popularized among
photochemists;15 however, the method failed to cleave a tough
reductively demanding bond, such as C(sp2)−Cl of 4-
chloroanisole. Furthermore, the proposed photocatalyst radical
anion generated during Con-PET might not be the actual
species responsible for the electron transfer. Several recent
elegant studies suggested that either a solvated electron or a
different species generated from the radical anion was the
actual reducing species.16−19 Recently, a consecutive three-
photon absorption has been claimed to escalate the reducing
power of a BOMPY dye.20 An alternative strategy for
generating super-reductants involves electrochemically reduc-
ing a molecule in the first step and then photoexciting the in
situ-generated reduced species.21−24 Such a technique can
afford highly reducing species that are capable of breaking
difficult bonds.25−27 However, the constant application of a
highly biased potential can lead to the accumulation of reactive
intermediates near the electrode surface, causing detrimental
reactions. In this vein, the photochemical generation of highly
reducing species in low concentrations bears a considerable
advantage in terms of controllability and chemoselectivity in
chemical transformations. Taking these aspects into consid-
eration, simple organic molecules that could be super-
reductant under visible light excitation are an attractive
solution toward breaking recalcitrant chemical bonds.28−33
In turn, there is a constant pursuit for readily available
organic molecules that, under simple chemical manipulations,
can tune the excited-state redox behavior and showcase their
tremendous reducing ability. One of the elegant strategies
toward their formation is to make anions from easily
deprotonatable species,34,35 so that the ground-state oxidation

Despite sporadic examples of utilizing anions, we note that
simple hydrocarbons have not been utilized as super-
reductants, or their use as photocatalysts is scarce. Notably,
preparing a family of molecules employing a rational design
principle that may unlock previously inaccessible reactivity is a
significant challenge. We propose that the formation of anions
by abstracting deprotonatable protons from a hydrocarbon can
lead to the formation of super-reductants. In this report, we
disclose a new class of photocatalysts consisting of substituted
fluorene molecules that are extremely potent reductants in
their excited state. Five different 9-aryl-9H-fluorene derivatives
(1a−1e) have been synthesized to establish a structure−
property correlation in their reductant behavior. A study of
their detailed photophysics reveals their delayed fluorescence
behavior, which affords a significantly long-lived excited state
for electron transfer events. Their super-reducing behavior has
been exploited for breaking extremely challenging C−X (X =
Cl, −PO(OMe)2) bonds. In essence, the work demonstrates
the first use of simple aromatic hydrocarbons as super-
reductants, unleashing the tremendous potential of this family
of small molecules in steering important photochemical
transformations.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Photophysical and Electrochemical Properties
We chose the hydrocarbon 9-phenyl-9H-fluorene (1a) and
several of its derivatives: p-OMe-phenyl (1b), 2,4-dimethox-
yphenyl (1c), p-tBu-phenyl (1d), and p-CF3-phenyl (1e) due
to the ease of deprotonation of the 9-C-H (Scheme 2).
Notably, fluorenes are fluorescent molecules that have found
wide applications in polymers, electronic devices, and photo-
chromic materials; however, fluorene as a single-molecule
photocatalyst is truly scarce.41−45 Previously, we have shown
that the parent fluorene molecule generates bifluoronylidene,
and both engage in electron transfer reactions, resulting in an
amplification of reactivity.46 Intriguingly, the targeted class of
photocatalysts can be readily prepared with tunable redox
properties, since the p-position of the 9-Ph group can be
electronically modulated.
DFT calculations on the 9-Ph fluorene anion disclosed that
the C-9 carbon significantly contributes to HOMO and hence
further electronic regulation might be possible from this
position. We envisage that pushing the HOMO to an even
higher energy by introducing electron-donating groups at the
para position of the 9-Ph ring will make the anion more easily
oxidizable. In agreement with this assertion, the HOMO
energies of 1a−and 1b−were −5.03 and −5.01 eV,
respectively. Besides, placing a strongly electron-withdrawing
−CF3 group at the -para position of the 9-Ph substituent (1e−)
stabilized the HOMO to an energy of −5.18 eV. The electron-
rich nature of the anions may lead to extremely easy ground-
state oxidation, likely even at a negative potential. The
optimized geometry of 1a−in the presence of solvent also
disclosed that the phenyl ring is considerably twisted from the
plane of fluorene by an angle of 37° (Scheme 2b). Such
twisting hinders the communication of the fluorene-centered
anion with the phenyl substitution.47 In turn, frontier highest
occupied molecular orbitals (HOMO) and frontier lowest
unoccupied molecular orbitals (LUMO) become segregated in
different parts of the molecule. Indeed, for 1a−the HOMO−1
orbital was centered on the fluorene, whereas the LUMO+1
orbital was completely spatially separated in the phenyl
substituent (SI, Figure S41). Such separation in the frontier
orbital is amenable to introduce a charge transfer (CT)
characteristic in the molecule upon light absorption. The
twisting of the appended ring from the fluorene plane is a
common feature for all the aromatic rings considered in this
study, and its extent is dependent on the steric clash with
fluorene-1,8-hydrogens. Accordingly, when the computation-
ally optimized structure of 1c−was analyzed, a twisting angle of
47° for the 2,4-dimethoxyphenyl ring from the fluorene plane
was recorded.

9-aryl-substituted fluorenes. Notably, the molecules can be

Next, we investigated the electrochemical behavior of these
anions. The ground-state oxidation of 1a−is located at −0.56
V (vs Ag+/Ag) as a peak potential (irreversible), and the
analogous oxidation wave for 1b−shows up at −0.64 V (vs
Ag+/Ag).
As is intuitive, the electron-rich methoxy group at the para
position of the phenyl ring makes the species even more
electron-rich, so that ground-state oxidation can be favored by
80 mV. As we will demonstrate in the following discussion, this
entire class of molecules qualifies as a super-reductant;
however, fine-tuning of their redox potential is possible by
modifying the electronic properties of the substituents.
Interestingly, the anion generated from 2,4-dimethoxyphenyl
fluorene (1c) shows a reversible ground-state redox wave at
−0.54 V vs Ag+/Ag (Figure 2c). To interrogate the reversible
redox wave in 1c−further, we attempted to correlate the width
of the redox wave with the scan rate. As illustrated in Figure
2d, the width of the redox wave consistently decreased with
increasing scan speed. A linear relationship between Ip and the
square root of the scan rate (υ1/2) following the Randles−
Ševčík equation strongly suggests the electrochemical behavior
of the molecule to be diffusion-limited (Figure 2e).49 This is
also indicative of the electron transfer from the electrode
surface to be fast and the structural rearrangement between
11c−and 21c• to be minimal.50 An electron-rich 4-tBu-phenyl-
substituted 1d−makes the ground-state oxidation further
easier, showcasing an irreversible electrochemical oxidation at
−0.56 V vs Ag+/Ag. In comparison, the electron-withdrawing
−CF3 group at the para position of phenyl (1e−) makes
ground-state oxidation slightly less facile. A cathodic shift of
250 mV was recorded compared to that of 1a−, displaying an
irreversible oxidation wave at −0.27 V vs Ag+/Ag. Having a
clear understanding of the ground-state redox behavior of the
fluorene molecule class, we set out to evaluate their excited-
state lifetimes, as a longer excited-state lifetime can significantly
aid in diffusion-controlled electron transfer processes. The
excited-state lifetime for 1a−, by a time-correlated single-
photon counting (TCSPC) measurement, exhibits a long
lifetime. The fluorescence decay data can be fit monoexponen-
tially, displaying a lifetime value of 43.3 ns. The long-lived
nature of the excited state suggests that a delayed component
may be present in the excited-state lifetime, and it is likely that
the anionic molecule reaches its triplet state. To investigate the
delayed lifetime, we collected luminescence data by introduc-
ing a suitable time delay after light excitation. Interestingly, the
same emission spectrum, however with diminished intensity,
was observed after 10−40 μs time delays. From this
experiment, the delayed lifetime component for 1a−was
calculated to be 19.2 μs (see SI, Section 13b).
Both lifetimes are longer and compare favorably to that of a
very popular photocatalyst, 4-CzIPN, which displays prompt
and delayed lifetimes of 18.7 and 1390 ns, respectively.
However, the latter molecule only offers moderate reducing
ability (Eox* = −1.04 V vs SCE).51 The presence of a delayed
lifetime for 1a−in the microsecond regime proves the presence
of a triplet state contribution in the lifetime. To trace the
population of the triplet state, we further attempted to examine
the phosphorescence of the molecule at 77 K. Very
interestingly, the molecule in the glass state of DMSO displays
luminescence, which is red-shifted by 57 nm compared to its
fluorescence spectrum at the same temperature. Given the
significantly different nature of the emission at 77 K compared
to room temperature emission, we attribute the former to
phosphorescence, and its appearance provides unequivocal
proof of the accessibility of the triplet state (Figure S34). From
the fluorescence and phosphorescence data at 77 K, singlet (S)
and triplet (T) state energies of 1a‑ were calculated to be 2.42
and 2.20 eV, respectively. A very small S-T gap of 0.22 eV
indicates the population distribution between the two states via
reverse intersystem crossing at RT,52,53 whose details we would
like to interrogate in the future. The presence of a delayed
component in the excited-state lifetime was also evident in 1c−,
and it was recorded to be 15.6 μs. A comparative list of
absorption and emission features and their excited-state
lifetimes for the entire class of molecules is presented in
Table 1.
Gathering the ground-state electrochemical potential and
the light absorption and emission data, we set out to estimate
the excited-state redox potentials for the proposed photo-
catalysts. To measure the optical gap (Eoo), we selected the
intersecting point of the absorption and emission spectra of the
anionic fluorene molecules. Such an intersecting point for 1a−
corresponds to a wavelength of 517 nm that translates into a
photon energy of 2.40 eV. The alteration of the ground-state
potential by this Eoo value revealed an excited-state potential of
−2.96 V vs Ag+/Ag or −3.01 V vs SCE. Such a highly negative
excited-state redox value suggests its tremendous excited-state
reducing ability, which will classify this molecule as a super-
reductant. Similar treatment on 1b−also reveals the optical gap
Eoo value of 2.33 eV, which translates into its excited-state
redox potential as −3.02 V vs SCE. Analogously, the excited-
state potential of 1c−was estimated as −2.96 V vs SCE (Table
1). Such a high reducing power of the excited state in
conjunction with long-lived excited states is a rare combina-
tion, and these features place these substituted fluorene anions
as a unique class of super-reductants. We note that, since for
the majority of the molecules the ground state redox potential
is irreversible in nature, the excited-state reductive capability

The excited-state potential of 1a−clearly suggests that
reductively demanding aryl chloride bonds can be easily
cleaved to generate aryl radical species. To investigate the
nature of excited-state quenching, we selected 4-chloroanisole
as the quenching substrate and studied the fluorescence
quenching of 1a−with a gradual increase in quencher
concentration. Notably, increasing the quencher concentration
continued to decrease the fluorescence intensity of the
fluorophore, following a linear Stern−Volmer pattern (Figure
3). This pattern suggests that excited-state electron transfer
occurs from the fluorene anion to the quencher. A plot of I0/I
with increasing quencher concentration reveals a very high
value of Ksv, 86540 M−1. Such a large value suggests that this
electron transfer may not be dynamic in nature. To detect
whether static quenching is present, we further conducted the
experiment by plotting τo/τ against the quencher concen-
tration. Such a plot revealed a flat straight line, indicating
predominantly static quenching.54 Many prior studies by
Barham and coworkers have identified ground-state interaction
between the photocatalyst and substrate molecules to claim
such quenching.55 However, this proposed ground-state
interaction was not very discernible in our UV−vis experiment
owing to the moderate quencher concentration, which
hindered its detection. It is probable that the weak interaction
of aryl chloride and the fluorene anion is best represented by
the “sphere of action” model,58 where the proximity of the
quenchers to the photocatalyst facilitates easy electron transfer.
Furthermore, we performed a temperature-dependent S−V
quenching experiment and found that the slope of the plot
decreased gradually with an increase in temperature (Figure
S26). However, steady-state quenching with high quencher
concentration (4 mM to 0.01 M, which is very close to the
substrate concentration in representative reactions) shows
deviation from linearity and an upward curvature (Figure S11).
Likely, at this high concentration regime, dynamic quenching
may also be a contributor. The electron transfer between the
PC and aryl chloride substrate is thermodynamically favorable,
as the driving force for the reductive cleavage of aryl chloride
in the case of PC 1a−was estimated to be −0.12 eV.
Application in SET Reactions
After establishing the super-reductant behavior of the
substituted fluorene molecules, we attempted to apply these
super-reductants to important synthetic transformations.
Initially, we attempted a few hydrodehalogenation reactions
of aryl chlorides.56 Ideally, the C−Cl bond homolysis of an aryl
chloride will generate a chloride anion and an aryl radical,
which can be intercepted by an H atom donor. As a source of
H atom donors, γ-terpinene was used so that the hydro-
dehalogenated product can be isolated. The reductive power of
the photoexcited 1a−was exploited to conduct hydro-
dehalogenation over a series of substrate molecules involving
a C−Cl bond. The p-chloroanisole and xylyl chloride under 10
mol % loading of 1a−afforded the hydrodehalogenated
products (3a−3b) in 80% and 67% yields, respectively
(Scheme 3). Analogously, a challenging substrate 3,5-
dimethoxy chlorobenzene (Ered = −2.94 V vs SCE)11 was
hydrodehalogenated in 72% isolated yield (3c). 4-Methyl and
4-ethyl chlorobenzenes were similarly hydrodehalogenated
under the reaction protocol, affording the respective products
(3d−3e) in 78% and 79% yields, respectively.
A heterocyclic thiophenyl chloride was hydrodehalogenated
to result in thiophene in a 62% yield. Moreover, p-cyano
chlorobenzene, p-methoxy α-naphthyl chloride, and 2,4-
ditrifluorochlorobenzene all underwent smooth hydrodehalo-
genation reactions, yielding the desired products (3g−3i) in
yields of 76−82%. After this, we focused on developing a C−C
cross-coupling methodology adopting the radical pathway,
where the first step of the process necessitates reductive
cleavage of the C(sp2)−Cl bond. The p-methoxy biphenyl,
biphenyl, p-tBu-biphenyl, and p-OtBu biphenyl products (4a−
4d) were synthesized from aryl chloride substrates in high
yields by adopting this methodology. As a base employed in
this cross-coupling reaction, the role of KOtBu is important
since the reaction fails when the base is changed to NaOtBu.
This observation may tentatively support the potassium-
mediated weak interaction between the fluorene anion and
the substrate (vide supra). In addition, NaOtBu may not be
very efficient in generating the phenyl cyclohexadienyl radical
anion via deprotonation. To assess the efficacy of 1c−in this
cross-coupling reaction, p-chloroanisole was used as the
substrate, which afforded 4a in 76% yield. The 2,4-
ditrifluoromethyl chlorobenzene, 2,4-dimethoxy chloroben-
zene, and α-naphthyl chloride successfully underwent cross-
coupling with benzene to afford the respective biphenyl
products (4f−4h) in yields ranging from 63% to 84%. Next,
3,5-dimethyl chlorobenzene underwent cross-coupling with
benzene, giving 4i in 76% yield. The same product was
obtained in a 71% yield when 1c was used as the catalyst for
the reaction. Multiple heterocyclic chlorides were also
successfully cross-coupled with benzene to yield the desired
products. Accordingly, thiophene and N-methylpyrrole were
successfully cross-coupled to p-chloroanisole, affording the
respective biphenyl products (4k−4l) in 72−78% isolated
yield. Similarly, thiophene is cross-coupled with p-chloroben-
zene to afford 2-phenylthiophene, 4m in 76% yield. Next, 2-
thiophenyl chloride was coupled in thiophene solvent to afford
2,2’-bisthiophene, 4n in 69% yield. Similarly, 2,5-dimethyl-
phenyl chloride was cross-coupled with furan to synthesize 4o

We have proved that fluorene anions (1a−−1d−) are super-
reducing at their excited state, which is required to break such
strong aryl chloride or aryl phosphonate bonds. Dimsyl anion
can be formed in the reaction medium DMSO; however, its
reducing power is limited and may not cleave the strong bonds
in these substrates.59,60
Reaction Intermediate and Catalytic Cycle
To gather further details of the electron transfer to a substrate
molecule and the reaction mechanism, we attempted to trace a
series of intermediates. The super-reducing anion 1a−or 1c−
reductively cleaves an aryl chloride bond via a single electron
transfer, upon which an aryl radical is generated. To further
prove that the cross-coupled product forms from single-
electron transfer-based bond cleavage, rather than any
photobase, we performed a Bunnett−Creary experiment
using 4,4′-dichlorobenzene as the probe molecule.61 At the
early phase of the reaction, terphenyl was detected as the sole
product without the formation of a monosubstituted product.
The formation of the disubstituted product and the
suppression of the monosubstitution product affirm the
involvement of electron transfer and a phenyl cyclohexadienyl
radical anion in the reaction mechanism. Such a strongly
reducing radical anion often facilitates a chain propagation step
(vide infra).
As proof of aryl radical generation, the radical was
intercepted as a TEMPO adduct, and the intermediate was
detected by high-resolution ESI-MS. During the electron
transfer, the catalyst 1c−forms fluorene radical 1c•, which is a
reasonably stable radical.62 The rigidity of the fluorene
framework and the dibenzylic nature of the radical confer
stability to these radicals.63 To characterize 1c• further, we
chemically oxidized 1c−by AgOTf and isolated a brown-
colored material. A THF solution of 1c• was examined by X-
band EPR spectroscopy at room temperature, and an organic
radical was evident with a g-value of 2.005 (Figure S32, SI).
Gratifyingly, this radical is reasonably stable under inert
conditions as evident from UV−vis spectroscopy, since no
change was discernible even after 12 h of its generation. The
fluorenyl radical stability has been highlighted earlier by Kubo,
although with a much more extended aromatic backbone.58
We chose the C−C cross-coupling reaction as a model and
describe the mechanism for such a catalytic process. Upon aryl
radical formation by SET from 1c−, the aryl radical I attacks
(hetero)arene, which is taken at a high concentration, as a
solvent. The formed intermediate II is easily deprotonated
under the basic conditions of the reaction medium to garner a
phenyl cyclohexadienyl radical anion, III (Figure 4). Such a
species (Eox = −2.50 V vs SCE) would easily reduce 1c• (Ered =
−0.59 V vs SCE) to bring the anionic catalyst back. Despite
this thermodynamic feasibility of catalyst regeneration, cross-
coupling may proceed predominantly by the chain propagation
step (vide infra). Intermediate III likely directly reduces an aryl
halide, generating an aryl radical. We interrogate the
contribution of such a step further by the measurement of
reaction quantum yield (QY).64 Interestingly, using 1c as the
catalyst, the QY was evaluated to be 1.15 (see details in Section
S16, SI). Once repeated with catalyst 1a, the average reaction
QY for the C−C cross-coupling reaction was evaluated as 0.8.
These QY values further suggest that there could be a
contribution from the chain propagation step, whereas only a
minor amount of product can be gathered via the catalytic
loop. To evaluate product formation through a chain
propagation step, we conducted an intermittent illumination
experiment, changing the LED pulse in the millisecond regime.
When the product yield was plotted against log(ton), where ton
is the pulse duration, we found a nonlinear dependence
between the rate of photon delivery and the product yield
(Figure 4b). Such behavior evidences the presence of a chain

To assess the stability of the catalyst under the reaction
conditions, we conducted a control reaction. The C−C cross-
coupling reaction with p-chloroanisole was performed under
white light irradiation for 6 h, and at the end, the amount of
catalyst in the reaction mixture was quantified to be 20%. This
observation likely suggests gradual photodegradation of the
catalyst under the reaction conditions. Assistance from a short
chain propagation step is quite common in radical-promoted
cross-coupling reactions, which we have already supported by
the intermittent illumination experiment. In this regard, the
cross-coupling reaction can be thought to follow the popular
base-promoted homolytic aromatic substitution (BHAS)
mechanism.67,68 In the BHAS mechanism, the base plays a
crucial role as the deprotonation of an intermediate is essential
to sustain chain propagation through the reductive cleavage of
a substrate by the putative intermediate. In the present case, we
found that 3 equiv of base are optimum, and decreasing its
amount reduces the yield. The dependence of the yield on the
amount of base further supports the BHAS mechanism. To
prove that the cross-coupling reaction starts strictly by a
reductive cleavage reaction and does not follow a benzyne
mechanism involving either KOtBu or fluorene anion as a
strong base,69,70 we chose a probe substrate, 2,6-dimethyl
chlorobenzene. Interestingly, in this reaction, some desired
cross-coupled product (8%) was obtained along with biphenyl
(22%). In addition, meta-xylene was also formed as a volatile
byproduct. The typical 3:1 ratio of biphenyl and the cross-
coupled product provided convincing evidence that the
reaction did not stop due to blocking adjacent sites of the
C−Cl bond and hence fully discards the benzyne mechanism
in the context of cross-coupling reactions. To discuss the fate
of the PC after electron transfer, no dimerization of the
catalysts 1b−1e is expected, since the para position of the 9-
aryl substituent is blocked. However, if the position is
unsubstituted, such as in the 9-phenyl analogue 1a, a small
amount of dimerization may occur.71 We detected such a trace
of a dimerized catalyst by high-resolution mass spectrometry.
Dimer formation for 1a was too low to be isolated and hence
was not detrimental.
■CONCLUSIONS
This report showcases that a simple aromatic hydrocarbon,
such as 9-phenyl-9H-fluorene molecules and related deriva-
tives, can behave as a class of super-reductants upon
deprotonation. Prior to this report, simple aromatic hydro-
carbons were never investigated as super-reductants. Initial
clues from DFT calculations suggested that decorating the 9-
Ph substituents may tune the ground-state potential in favor of
extremely easy oxidation, which may translate into the super-
reducing behavior of these molecules at their excited state.
Encouragingly, the appropriate substituents can span a highly
reducible range of −2.74 to −3.04 V versus SCE, showing
great promise in reductive bond cleavage chemistry for
challenging substrates. Another significant advantage of this
class of photocatalysts is their extended excited-state lifetime.
The shorter components are usually tenths of nanoseconds
long-lived, while there is a delayed component that is long-
lived by a few μs. It is anticipated that the delayed component
is attributed to the triplet state of the anionic molecules. To
harness the super-reducing power of anionic photocatalysts,
several hydrodehalogenation, C−C cross-coupling reactions,
and C−P bond reactions have been examined. The stability of
the intermediate upon SET from the fluorene anion has been
examined to prove that it is a stable radical intermediate and
that its one-electron reduction is thermodynamically feasible,
although the chain propagation step contributes significantly to
the catalytic cycle. This work demonstrates the power of easily
synthesized hydrocarbons that, after deprotonation, exhibit
tremendous reducing power in their excited states.
■MATERIALS AND METHODS
Preparation of Catalysts
All 9-aryl-9H-fluorene derivatives were synthesized following a
literature procedure after some modifications.61 In an oven-
dried, argon-purged round-bottom flask, the Grignard solution
was prepared from magnesium turnings (15 mmol) reacting
with arylbromo derivatives (10 mmol) in anhydrous THF (30
mL). In another Schlenk flask, fluorenone (8 mmol) was
dissolved in anhydrous THF (15 mL), cooled to 0 °C, and
then the freshly prepared Grignard solution was added
dropwise to the chilled fluorenone solution over a period of
20 min. The mixture was then allowed to come to room
temperature and stirred for 24 h. After completion, the
reaction was quenched by the addition of saturated aqueous
ammonium chloride (40 mL) and then extracted three times
with dichloromethane (3 × 40 mL). The combined organic
layers were dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, and the solvent was
evaporated under reduced pressure. The residue was then
purified by flash column chromatography on silica gel to yield
fluoren-9-ol as a colorless liquid.
In the subsequent step, the liquid was transferred into a 100
mL round-bottom flask and dissolved in 30 mL of DCM. To
this solution, 1.5 equiv of triethylsilane and 1.6 equiv of
trifluoroacetic acid were added. The solution was stirred for 30
min at room temperature. Upon completion, the reaction was
quenched by the addition of water, and the mixture was
extracted three times with dichloromethane. The combined
organic layers were dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, and the
solvent was evaporated under reduced pressure. The product
was purified by column chromatography using hexane as an
eluent. The products were obtained as white solids.
