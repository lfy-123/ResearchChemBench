S1. Experimental section 
S1.1. Materials and methods 
 
All the solvents were purchased from commercial sources and were used without further purification. 
Merck 60 F254 aluminium-coated plates were used for Thin Layer Chromatography to assess the 
progression of the reaction. The NMR studies were conducted on a Bruker AV-III 400 or 500 MHz 
spectrometer with DMSO-d6/CDCl3 as solvent and TMS as internal standards. Chemical shifts are 
expressed in parts per million (δ-scale), while coupling constants are reported in hertz (Hz). 1H NMR 
splitting patterns are abbreviated using the following labels: "s" for singlet, "d" for doublet, "t" for triplet, 
and "m" for multiplet. FT-IR spectra were acquired in the 4000-400 cm–1 region using a Shimadzu 8400 
FT-IR spectrometer. The mass spectrum was generated using a Shimadzu mass spectrometer. Melting 
points were recorded on a melting point apparatus by open capillary method. 
S1.2. General procedure for synthesis 
S1.2.1 General procedure for synthesis of 4,6-dichloro-2H-pyridazin-3-one 2 
 
A solution of 3,4,6-trichloropyridazine 1 (1 mmol) in glacial acetic acid (10 mL) was heated under 
reflux for 3 h. Upon completion, the reaction mixture was allowed to cool to ambient temperature and 
then filtered to remove any insoluble components. The filtrate was diluted with ethyl acetate and 
repeatedly rinsed with excess water, followed by brine solution. The organic layer was dried over 
anhydrous magnesium sulfate, filtered, and concentrated under reduced pressure. The crude residue 
was refined by column chromatography using 30% ethyl acetate in hexane, resulting in 4,6-dichloro-2H-
pyridazin-3-one 2 as a solid in 38% yield.  
S1.2.2 General procedure for synthesis of 2-benzyl-4,6-dichloropyridazin-3(2H)-one 3 
 
A stirred solution of 4,6-dichloro-2H-pyridazin-3-one 2 (1 mmol) and potassium carbonate (3 mmol) 
in dry N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) was kept at room temperature for 2 h to allow for complete 
deprotonation. Consequently, benzyl bromide (1 mmol) was added dropwise, and the reaction mixture 
was agitated overnight at ambient temperature. Upon completion (monitored by TLC), the reaction was 
quenched by the addition of water and extracted with ethyl acetate. The mixed organic layers were 
washed with brine solution, dried over anhydrous magnesium sulfate, filtered, and concentrated under 
reduced pressure. The crude product was purified by column chromatography using a suitable ethyl 
acetate/hexane gradient to obtain the 2-benzyl-4,6-dichloropyridazin-3(2H)-one 3 in 70% yield.

To a stirred solution of the azetidine-3-carboxylic acid derivative 4 (1 mmol) in DMF (10 mL), N,N-
diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA, 2 mmol) and HATU (1 mmol) were added progressively. The reaction 
mixture was agitated at room temperature for 15 minutes to activate the acid. Subsequently, the 
corresponding amine (1 mmol) was added, and the mixture was agitated for the next 18 h at ambient 
temperature. Upon completion, the reaction was diluted with ethyl acetate (10 mL) and washed 
successively with 1 N HCl, saturated NaCl solution, followed by water. The organic layer was dried, 
concentrated under reduced pressure, and the residue was used for the next step. 
S1.2.4 General procedure for synthesis of azetidine-3-carboxamide derivatives 6a-6j 
 
To a solution of the protected amide intermediate 5a-5j (1 mmol) in dichloromethane (DCM), 
trifluoroacetic acid (TFA, 5 mmol) was added dropwise, and the reaction mixture was agitated at room 
temperature for 18 h. After completion, excess 1 N NaOH was added to neutralize the reaction mixture, 
followed by vigorous stirring for 15 minutes. The organic phase was separated, dried over anhydrous 
magnesium sulfate, filtered, and concentrated under reduced pressure to be used in the next step. 
S1.2.5 General procedure for synthesis of 1-(2-benzyl-6-chloro-3-oxo-2,3-dihydropyridazin-4-yl)-
N-(aryl)azetidine-3-carboxamide AZ1-AZ10 
 
A combination of 2-benzyl-4,6-dichloropyridazin-3(2H)-one 3 (1 mmol) and deprotected amine 6a-6j 
(1.2 mmol) in DMF was treated with DIPEA (1.5 mmol) at room temperature. The reaction mixture was 
stirred for 16 h. After completion, the reaction was subsequently quenched by dumping it into ice-cold 
water. The resultant product was recovered by filtering, washing with water, followed by trituration with 
cold diethyl ether to provide the final coupled product AZ1-AZ10 as a solid in 75-81 % yield.

1.3. In vitro antimicrobial study 
 
In accordance with defined procedures, the scaffolds have been examined for their in vitro 
antimicrobial activity against gram-positive bacteria (S. aureus and S. pyogenes), gram-negative 
bacteria (E. coli and P. aeruginosa), and fungal strain (C. albicans and A. niger) by the broth dilution 
method.[35-37] Through the course of the experiment, the concentration of the screening molecules AZ1-
AZ10 was varied from 62.5 µg/mL to 500 µg/mL for antibacterial and 250 µg/mL to 1000 µg/mL for 
antifungal activity. As a standard medication, chloramphenicol has been utilized to perform biological 
activity against both gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria. Moreover, griseofulvin has been utilized 
as a conventional medication for antifungal therapy.  
1.4. In-silico study 
1.4.1. Quantum chemical calculations 
 
Molecular geometry optimization of synthesized scaffolds was done using density functional theory 
(DFT) in the gas phase. The DFT computations were done using Becke 3 Lee Yang par[38] (B3LYP) in 
combination with 6-31G(d) as basis sets by Gaussian 16 software suite.[39] Frontier molecular orbitals 
(FMOs’), energy gap associated with the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest 
unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) were determined by adopting B3LYP/6-31G(d) method.[40] Gauss 
View 6.0 [41] was tailored for the development of the structure and the graphic representation of HOMO 
and LUMO for DFT computational investigation.

1.4.2. Prediction of ADMET 
 
The pharmacokinetic and drug-likeness profiles of the azetidine-linked pyrazine based heterocyclic 
scaffolds AZ1-AZ10 were examined using the in-silico tools SwissADME. These tools predict 
absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) characteristics.  
 
1.4.3. Molecular docking 
 
Molecular docking modelling of AZ1-AZ10 molecules was performed with DNA gyrase (PDB: 
5MMN) and CYP51 (PDB: 3JUV) receptors to identify the conceptual best binding efficiency by Molegro 
Virtual Docker (MVD-2013). The crystal structures of these receptors were collected from the RCSB 
protein data bank repository (https://www.rcsb.org). The optimized compounds AZ1-AZ10 served as 
input to execute the docking technique. The receptors and investigated scaffolds were prepared by 
Molegro Virtual Docker. In addition, MolDock scores were used as scoring function and grid parameters 
were set as X= -46.26, Y= 4.41 and Z= 3.66 (PDB: 5MMN) and X= -75.99, Y= -27.92 and Z= -7.60 
(PDB: 3JUV) with a radius of approximately 15.00 Å. Eventually, MVD was employed to dock each of 
the co-crystallized ligands and the produced compounds into the enzyme binding site.[42,43] The docking 
methodology is affirmed by re-docking the co-crystallized ligand onto the receptor pocket with an RMSD 
= 1.192 Å, which is less than 2.0 Å, demonstrating that the docking process was befittingly accurate 
(Figure S55).[44-45] The resulting top poses were deposited for post-docking analysis. UCSF ChimeraX 
[46] and Discovery Studio Visualizer 21 [47] have been applied to showcase the interactions of the docking 
data.
