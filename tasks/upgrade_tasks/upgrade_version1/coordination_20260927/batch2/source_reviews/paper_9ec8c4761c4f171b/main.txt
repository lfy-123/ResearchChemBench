A B S T R A C T
An approach to the synthesis of 4-biarylhydrazinylidenepyrazolon-3-ones as new dyes via the Suzuki cross-coupling of 4-halophenylhydrazinylidenepyrazolones with 
arylboronic acids was developed. The UV spectra recorded in CHCl3 and DMF for 4-biarylhydrazinylidenepyrazolones was found to have a bathochromic shift of the 
longwave band maxima as well as in an increase in the molar absorption coefficient comparing to monoaryl-substituted analogs. QM calculations confirm these 
experimental data since 4-biarylhydrazinylidenepyrazolones have smaller band gap values. CF3-substituted 4-biarylhydrazinylidenepyrazolonones were revealed to 
have a hypsochromic shift of the absorption maxima of the longwave band in DMF vs CHCl3 in contrast to non-fluorinated analogs. The obtained pyrazolones are 
characterized by solid-state emission with a quantum yield of up to 10 % and an emission time of more than 1 ns. The cyclic voltammograms of 5-CF3-2-phenyl-4- 
biarylhydrazinylidenepyrazolones have the irreversible reducing nature owing to the 2e−/2H+ process. The novel 4-biarylhydrazinylidenepyrazolonone dyes can be 
used for the fluorescent visualization of the rhabdomyosarcoma cells. In addition, these compounds have a promising antigonorrheal activity.
1. Introduction
Azo dyes are known to be the largest group of produced colorants 
among all organic dyes (about 70 %) [1]. The prevalence of azo dyes is 
due to the simplicity of approaches to their synthesis, while they are 
distinguished by the possibility of a wide variation in structural frag­
ments. In addition, they have a high molar absorption coefficient and 
good stability to both light and moisture. The group of azo dyes are 
including 4-arylhydrazinylidenepyrazol-5-one (AHP) derivatives, which 
applied as not only dyes, but also pigments and mordant, for example, 
the most-known a lemon-yellow food coloring Tartrazine (Acid Yellow 
23, C.I. 19140) [E102] [2–4], textile dyes Acid Yellow 17 (C.I. 18965), 
Acid Yellow 76 (C.I. 18850), chrome wool dyes Mordant Yellow 30 (C.I. 
18710), Mordant Red 7 (C.I. 18760) and Mordant Red 19 (C.I. 18735) 
(Fig. 1) [3]. Sudan Yellow 3G (Solvent Yellow 16, C.I. 12700) is used as a 
pigment in cosmetics and toner for printers, a dye for inks, and applied 
to produce yellow smoke in pyrotechnics [3]. Pigment Yellow 10 (C.I. 
12710) is applied for road-marking paints [5].
AHPs as azo dyes continue to gain the attention of researchers [6–16]. A 
study of their photophysical properties [17] showed that AHPs can exhibit 
solvatochromism [6,12,18,19] due to their azo-hydrazone, keto-enol and 
cis-trans isomerism that can be used in sensors and in electronics for 
molecular switches construction [20]. Another characteristic of AHPs is 
halochromism [6,18], which contributes to the possibility of using these 
compounds as indicators, including for determining the pH of solutions 
[21]. AHPs were shown to be revealed the properties of chemosensors 
owing to their decolorization. For eхample, 4-((2,4-dimethylphenyl) 
diazenyl)-2-(naphthalen-1-yl)-5-phenyl-2,4-dihydropyrazol-3-one) can be 
applied to determine Cu2+ ions in micromolar concentrations [22], 
4-(4-(2-(2-aminophenyl)hydrazinylidene)-3-methyl-5-oxo-4,5-dihydropyr­
azol-1-yl)benzenesulfonic acid can be used for the qualitative detection of 
formaldehyde [9]. The introduction of ortho-carboxyl group with strong 
coordination ability in arylhydrazone moiety promotes dye-metal 
complexation [23]. Besides the azo dyes having pyrazolone scaffold are 
widely used to develop the organic species with pharmacological properties 
especially antibacterial, antiviral and cytotoxic activities [24]. Also, there 
are some arylazopyrazolone dyes having potent antimicrobial effect [25].
No information on the development of dyes based on 4-arylhy­
drazinylidene-5-(polyfluoroalkyl)pyrazol-3-ones (4-AHPPs) has been 
found. Although it is known that the introduction of electronegative 
fluorine atoms into the structure of organic molecules affects their 
physicochemical and biological properties [26–34]. Recently we have

Considering the great potential of pyrazolones as potential antimicrobial 
agents, we studied their antibacterial and antifungal activity. To extend 
the aryl chain, we propose to use two approaches (Scheme 1). The first 
method is based on the Pd-catalyzed Suzuki cross-coupling reaction of 2- 
arylhydrazinylidene-3-oxo esters with arylboronic acids and subsequent 
cyclization of the resulting compounds with substituted hydrazines. In 
the second, the target pyrazolones can be obtained by the cross-coupling 
reaction of arylboronic acids with pre-synthesized 4-(4-haloaryl) 
hydrazinylidene-5-CF3-pyrazol-3-ones.
2. Results and discussion
2.1. Chemistry
2.1.1. Synthesis and structure of 4-biarylhydrazinylidenepyrazolones
To implement the first approach, ethyl-4,4,4-trifluoro-2-[2-(4- 
iodophenyl)hydrazinylidene]-3-oxobutanoate 1a and trifluoro-2-[2-(4- 
iodophenyl)hydrazinylidene]-1-phenylbutane-1,3-dione 1b were syn­
thesized from the commercial trifluoroacetate ester and trifluoro-1- 
phenylbutane-1,3-dione through the azo coupling reaction with 4-ioda­
niline based on a known method [32,36–39]. The compound 1b was 
obtained for subsequent comparative study of the 1,3-dicarbonyl com­
pounds of different types in cross-coupling reactions. The Suzuki 
cross-coupling reaction of ester 1a with phenylboronic acid 2a led to the 
product mixture formation. According to a gas chromatography-mass 
spectroscopy (GC/MS) analysis, this mixture contained 3-[2-(biphe­
nyl-4-yl)hydrazinylidene]-1,1,1-trifluoropropan-2-one 3 as the main 
product, which was apparently formed due to the ketone decomposition 
of the intermediate A by the action of the base K2CO3 (Scheme 2). An 
attempt to introduce diketone 1b as another type of 1,3-dicarbonyl re­
agent into a similar cross-coupling reaction was also unsuccessful due to 
even greater resinification. In this case, the main product was 
2-[2-(biphenyl-4-yl)hydrazinylidene]-1-phenylethan-1-one 4 as a result 
of acidic cleavage of intermediate B. The molecular ions of products 3, 4 
indicated that the cross-coupling reactions occurred at the arylhy­
drazone fragment, but the 1,3-dicarbonyl skeleton itself was not stable 
under the synthesis conditions and underwent destruction depending on 
the nature of substituents (please see corresponding chromatograms 
with mass spectra in the Supplementary data). We did not isolate com­
pounds 3 and 4 in pure form because they would not lead to the target 
azopyrazolones.
The futility of the first approach forced us to investigate the second 
route (see Scheme 1). Using the previously described procedure [35], we 
synthesized CF3-pyrazolones 5a-d containing bromine and iodine atoms 
in the arylhydrazinylidene fragment, which are necessary for subsequent 
cross-coupling reactions, and iodine derivatives 5b,d were synthesized 
for the first time. In a similar manner, 4-[2-(4-iodophenyl)hydrazinylide­
ne]-5-methyl-2-phenylpyrazolone 5e was obtained for subsequent 
determination of the trifluoromethyl group effect on the photophysical 
properties of the new dyes. Next, to find the optimal conditions for the 
synthesis of 4-biarylhydrazinylidenepyrazolones 6a, 7a, we carried out a 
series of Suzuki cross-coupling reactions of 1 equiv. of 4-(4-haloaryl) 
hydrazinylidenepyrazolones 5a–c with 1.2 equiv. of phenylboronic acid 
2a in closed vials in an inert atmosphere (argon) upon heating in an 
EtOH/H2O mixture in the presence of 1.5 equiv. of K2CO3 as a base and 
tetrakis(triphenylphosphine)palladium (Pd(PPh3)4) catalyst, varying its 
content from 3 to 5 mol% (Table 1, Entries 1–12). An increase in the 
catalyst amount and the use of iodine-containing 4-arylhydrazinylidene­
pyrazol-3-ones 5b,d as a substrate was found to rise the yield of target 
pyrazolones 6a, 7a up to 57–59 %. The N-phenyl substituent in the pyr­
azolone core had virtually no effect on the yield of the products. Using of 
the Pd2(dba)3/phosphonium ligand catalytic system (XPhos –

(9,9-dimethyl-9H-xanthene-4,5-diyl)bis(diphenylphosphane), BINAP – 
(2,2′-bis(diphenylphosphino)-1,1′-binaphthyl)) in this reaction resulted 
in the incomplete conversion of the starting reagents, and the yield of the

Heterocycles 6–8 can undergo azo-hydrazone and keto-enol 
tautomerism, so they can exist as azo-enol (AE), azo-ketone (AK) and/ 
or hydrazone-ketone (HK) forms, and Z- and E-isomerism can be 
observed for HK form (Scheme 4). It has been previously shown that 
pyrazolones 5 are characterized by existence both in solutions and in 
solid form only as the Z-HK form [35]. Using X-diffraction analysis 
(XRD) and IR spectroscopy, we investigated the structure of the obtained 
compounds 6–8 in the solid phase (in single crystals or powders). To 
characterize their form in solutions, IR and NMR spectra measured in 
CDCl3 and DMSO‑d6 were used.
According to XRD data, heterocycle 6d (Fig. 2) exists in crystals as 
the Z-HK isomer stabilized by the intramolecular hydrogen bond (IHB) 
formation between the NH proton of the arylhydrazone substituent and 
the carbonyl oxygen atom of the pyrazole fragment (the H006⋯O1 
distance is 2.10(3) Å). In addition, the presence of the H2 proton in the 
pyrazolone core generates intermolecular hydrogen bonds with the O1′ 
oxygen atom (the H2⋯O1′ distance is 1.96(3) Å) to form dimeric chains 
of pyrazolone molecules 6d. It should also be noted that the C7–C12 
benzene ring lies in the same plane as the N1N2C3C4C5 pyrazolone 
core, while the plane of the C13–C18 ring is rotated relative to the rest of 
the molecule at an angle of 23.2◦. Non-fluorinated pyrazolone 8a exists 
in crystals as two crystallographically independent molecules, both in 
the form of Z-HK isomer (Fig. 3). The IHB between the NH proton of the 
arylhydrazone substituent and the carbonyl oxygen atom of the pyrazole 
fragment is H4⋯O1 2.14(4) Å and H003⋯O1A 2.01(4) Å, respectively. 
In compound 8a, the benzene ring C12–C16 and the pyrazolone core 
N1N2C3C4C5 are located in the same plane, while the C6–C11 and 
C18–C22 rings are rotated relative to the rest of the molecule at an angle 
of 8.8 and 29.2◦, and 14.3 and 22.6◦(for the second molecule with A 
numbers).

The experimental UV–Vis absorption spectra of the 5f,g, 6a-g, 7a-g, 
8a,b in CHCl3 and DMF have three bands in the 245–290 nm, 300–330 
nm and 400–470 nm ranges (Figs. 4 and 5). According to the literature 
data [51], the bands in the first two ranges could be assigned to the π-π* 
transition of the aromatic moieties, and the third broad band located at 
400–470 nm corresponds to π-π* transition involving the π-electrons of 
the azomethine groups. To confirm this, we performed QM calculations 
of the absorption spectra for all compounds by TDDFT at the 
CAM-B3LYP/6-311+G* level of theory in chloroform solvent (Table S3). 
Thus, two dominant transition types were identified. The first consists of 
intense π-π* transitions in the 354–404 nm range (fosc > 1.3 for 6–8 and 
fosc ~ 1 for 5f,g), where the S0→S1 excitation is dominated (a contri­
bution of >90 % in most cases) by the HOMO→LUMO transition, 
delocalized across the azomethine bridge. The second range at 224–261 
nm (fosc 0.1–0.6) can be attributed to π-π* transitions belonging mainly 
HOMO→LUMO+1 (>50 % in most cases) excitation of the phenyl(s) and 
pyrazolone rings (for more information, please see Table S4).
In addition, the calculated UV spectrum in CHCl3 of compound 7a in 
AE form has absorption maxima of longwave band at λmax 357 nm 
(Table S3, Fig. S16) while analogous band of HK tautomer reveals at λmax 
379 nm (Table S3, Fig. S17). This may also indicate that pyrazolones 6–8 
exist in solutions in the HK form.
2.1.4. Electrochemical properties
The electrochemical properties of new pyrazolones were studied 
using cyclic voltammetry on the example of compounds 7b,e, which 
have different substituents in the introduced aryl fragment (Fig. 11). 
Their cyclic voltammograms (CVs) in the cathodic region have the same 
character, which indicates that the electrode reduction process in an 
aprotic medium (CH2Cl2 with Bu4NPF6 as supporting electrolyte) occurs 
through the transfer of 2e−/2H+, leading to the hydrazine derivative. 
This process is reversible, since two peaks are observed during reverse 
scanning. The dominant reduction peaks centered on −1.48 V for 
compounds 7b (Fig. 11a) and −1.43 V for 7e (Fig. 11b) arise from the 
reduction of the hydrazono group. The mechanism of electrochemical 
reduction of azo compounds has been widely studied and is described by 
the two-electron reduction of the azo group to a hydrazine fragment 
[52]. The anodic regions of the CVs of compounds 7b,e are expectedly 
unlike due to the different nature of the substituents in the aromatic 
fragment. The CV of compound 7b has two peaks of irreversible pro­
cesses corresponding to the oxidation of the MeS-group at 0.99 V and the 
NH-group at 1.35 V. In contrast the CV of compound 7e has only one 
peak of quasi-reversible oxidation of NH-group at 1.25 V, since the 
tert-butyl moiety of compound 7e is resistant to oxidation processes.
2.2. Biological activity
2.2.1. Live cell visualization
Earlier, we showed that 4-arylhydrazinylidene-5-(polyfluoroalkyl) 
pyrazol-3-ones can be used for visualization of the Vero cells (African

compounds 6a,b,e, 7b,d,g for the fluorescent visualization of the

2.2.2. Antimicrobial activity
The need for new antibiotics is extremely high owing to the wide­
spread resistance of infectious disease pathogens to existing antibacte­
rial drugs. This is especially actual for the bacteria Neisseria gonorrhoeae, 
which has developed resistance to all antimicrobial drugs recommended 
for monotherapy of gonorrhea, including Ceftriaxone [58]. Recently we 
have found among polyfluoroalkyl-containing AHPs the powerful anti­
gonorrheal agents with the minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) to 
0.9 μg/mL [35], which was significantly higher than the activity of the 
reference Spectinomycin and was slightly less than that of Ceftriaxone.
In this work, the activity of the synthesized AHPPs 6, 7 was estimated 
in relation to the strain of Neisseria gonorrhoeae ATCC 49226/NCTC 
12700 (CTXS) and, for active compounds, against a clinical isolate 
N. gonorrhoeae 59 (CTXR). As shown in Table 5, from the group of NH- 
pyrazolones 6a-g, compounds 6a-d, f (MIC up to 0.24 μg/mL) have a 
high inhibitory effect against N. gonorrhoeae (CTXS), exceeding the ef­
fect of Spectinomycin by 9–90 times, and the activity of 4-MeS-contain­
ing derivative 6b is at the level of Ceftriaxone. The introduction of a 
phenyl group into the pyrazole core, in general, leads to a decrease in the 
antigonorrheal effect. Pyrazolones 7a-c exceed the activity of Specti­
nomycin by 10–20 times, while compound 7d is the most active in this 
series with an MIC of 0.24 μg/mL. Note that pyrazolone 5g having an 
monoarylhydrazinylidene fragment does not exhibit antigonorrheal 
activity.
For the leading compounds 6a, 6c, 6d, 6f, 7b, 7d, 7f, the inhibitory 
effect against the CTXR strain resistant to Ceftriaxone was evaluated. It 
was found that pyrazolones 6c, 6f, 7b, 7g are not active against this 
strain, whereas compounds 6a, 6d, 7d exhibit high inhibitory properties 
(MIC up to 0.12 μg/mL). The fact that the synthesized pyrazolones 6, 7 
are active against the CTXR strain allowed us to suggest that the 
mechanism of their antibacterial action may be due to interactions with 
other biotargets, which are in not typical for Ceftriaxone.
In addition, the antifungal activity of the compounds against strains 
of three pathogenic dermatophytes (Trichophyton rubrum, Epi­
dermophyton floccosum, Microsporum canis) and one strain of yeast-like 
fungi Candida albicans was evaluated. Fluconazole was used as posi­
tive control (MIC 1.56–3.12 μg/mL). All investigated compounds except 
pyrazolone 6a (MIC 25 μg/mL) did not show activity against C. albicans. 
This compound inhibited the growth of E. floccosum at MIC 12.5 μg/mL. 
Pyrazolone 6g showed weak activity against T. rubrum, E. floccosum 
(MIC 25 μg/mL), and M. canis (MIC 50 μg/mL).

3.3.1. XRD experiments

Diffraction Ltd., Abingdon, UK) diffractometer with a graphite mono­

sorption correction was applied. Using Olex2 [59], the structure was 
solved with the SHELXT [60] structure solution program using Intrinsic 
Phasing and refined with the SHELXL [61] refinement package using 
Least Squares minimization. All non-hydrogen atoms were refined in the 
anisotropic approximation; H-atoms at the C–H bonds were refined in 
the “rider” model with dependent displacement parameters. An empir­
ical absorption correction was carried out through spherical harmonics, 
implemented in the SCALE3 ABSPACK scaling algorithm by the program 
“CrysAlisPro” (Rigaku Oxford Diffraction).
The suitable single crystals of compound 6d were obtained by slow 
crystallization from ethanol. Main crystallographic data for 6d:

3.3.2. UV and photoluminescence spectra
UV–Vis spectra were recorded for 1 × 10−5 M CHCl3 and DMF so­
lutions with a Shimadzu UV-2600/UV-2401PC spectrophotometers. The 
luminescence and excitation spectra were recorded using a FS5 Edin­
burgh Instruments spectrofluorometer. UV–Vis and fluorescence spectra 
were recorded using standard 1 cm quartz cells at room temperature, 
non-degassed. The excitation and emission slits were set at 7–10 nm for 
solution measurements and 1–3 nm for solid samples measurements. 
Dilute solutions (A<0.01) were used to minimize the reabsorption ef­
fects for quantum yields and lifetimes measurements. Fluorescence 
spectra of solid powders were recorded using SC-10 module at room 
temperature. Absolute quantum yields were measured using SC-30 
integrating sphere at room temperature. The emission lifetimes have 
been measured using the TCSPC option of FS5 Edinburgh Instruments 
spectrofluorometer. The sample has been excited by EPL-450 ps pulsed 
diode laser centered at 450 or EPLED-300 ps pulsed light emitted laser 
centered at 300. The instrument response function (IRF) has been 
recorded under described conditions by replacing the sample with a 
silica diffuser. The time decay data have been analyzed by nonlinear 
least-squares fitting with deconvolution of the IRF using the Fluoracle 
software package.
3.3.3. Cyclic voltammetry
Cyclic voltammetry was carried out on a Metrohm Autolab 
PGSTAT302 N potentiostat with a standard three-electrode configura­
tion. Typically, a three-electrode cell equipped with a platinum disk 
working electrode (3 mm), a glass carbon disk counter electrode (3 mm), 
and an Ag/AgNO3 (0.01 M) pseudo-reference electrode was used. 
Measurements were made in dry CH2Cl2 with tetrabutylammonium 
hexafluorophosphate (0.1 M) as the supporting electrolyte under an 
argon atmosphere at a scan rate of 100 mV/s. The potential of reference 
electrode was calibrated by using the ferrocene/ferrocenium redox 
couple (Fc+/Fc).
3.3.4. QM calculations (Table S5)
Conformational search was carried out before DFT calculations using 
the AQME program [62]. DFT calculations were performed using the 
ORCA program (ver. 5.0.3) [40]. The ground-state geometry optimiza­
tions were performed at the B3LYP-D3(BJ)/6-311+G* level of theory in 
the gas phase and in chloroform solvent (under the conductor-like 
polarizable continuum model (C-PCM) [49]. Frequency analyses were 
carried out at the same theoretical level to ensure that the optimized 
geometries correspond to a local minimum on the potential energy 
surface; all compounds were characterized by only real vibrational fre­
quencies. The absorption spectra were calculated by TDDFT at the 
CAM-B3LYP/6-311+G* theoretical level in chloroform solvent.
3.3.5. Antifungal activity assay
The following fungal strains were used: Microsporum canis (RCPG F 
1643/1585), Trichophyton rubrum (RCPG F 1408), Epidermophyton floc­
cosum (RCPG F 1659/17) and yeast Candida albicans (RCPG Y 401/NCTC 
885/653). The fungal strains were obtained from Russian Collection of 
Pathogenic Fungi (RCPF), Kashkin Research Institute of Medical 
Mycology (St. Peterburg, Russia). Fungi were cultivated at 27 ◦C on 
Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA) and MIC was determined by using 
Sabouraud Dextrose Broth (SDB). The test and standard compounds 
were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 1/10) and applied in 
different concentrations (100–0.19 μg/mL). Dimethyl sulfoxide was 
used as a negative control and antifungal drug fluconazole as a positive 
control. The broth dilution test was performed in test tubes. The inoc­
ulum suspension, which gave the final concentration of 1 × 108 CFU/ 
mL, was prepared. A growth control tube and sterility control tube were 
used in each test. After 14 days incubation at 27 ◦C, MIC was determined 
visually as the lowest concentration that inhibited the growth, evi­
denced by the absence of turbidity.

3.4. Cell cultivation
The study was performed on RD and Vero cells obtained from the 
Russian collection of cell cultures of the Institute of Cytology, Russian 
Academy of Sciences. The cells were maintained in DMEM nutrient

the experiments.
3.5. Microscopy
Solutions of the tested substances were prepared in DMSO with 
concentrations of 1 × 10−3 M and diluted with complete nutrient me­
dium to a concentration of 1 × 10−5 M. The solutions were prepared 
immediately before adding the substances to the cells. All manipulations 
with solutions of substances and, subsequently, with cells were carried 
out in a Bioinnelix laminar box (BioinnLabs, Russia) under red illumi­
nation (625 nm) to minimize exposure of substances to light.
For the study, cells were seeded into glass-bottomed dishes for 
confocal microscopy (JetBiofil, China) at a concentration of 1 × 104 
cells/mL and incubated for a day, after which a solution of the test 
substances in complete nutrient medium was added. The cells were 
incubated with the substance for 30 min in the incubator, after which 
they were washed three times with nutrient medium to remove the dye 
that had not entered the cell and examined using a confocal microscope. 
When moving the samples to the confocal microscope room, the dishes 
were wrapped in aluminum foil to protect the cells from light exposure 
as much as possible.
For additional staining, the dye for lipid droplets BDP 650/665 
(Lumiprob, Russia), for mitochondria MitoTracker Red (Thermo Scien­
tific, USA) and for nucleus Hoechst 33342 (Lumiprob, Russia) was used. 
The dyes were used according to the manufacturer instructions, added in 
the required concentration (50, 20 and 25 nM, respectively) to the 
nutrient medium together with the test substance.
A LSM-710 laser scanning confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany) 
was used for the study. Cells were examined using an immersion 40×
objective. The focus was preliminarily adjusted using a 633 nm laser to 
minimally affect the cells, then lambda (full-spectral) images from other 
lasers were taken.
Fluorescence spectra were extracted from the lambda images using 
the PyLSM program (https://github.com/arteys/PyLSM) as well as 
ImageJ. Full color images were also reconstructed using PyLSM pro­
gram. It should be noted that the microscope is equipped with band-pass 
light filters, which lead to dips in the emission plots obtained with the 
confocal microscope. To evaluate the colocalization of the substances 
under study with commercial dyes, the Coloc2 plugin for ImageJ was 
used.
