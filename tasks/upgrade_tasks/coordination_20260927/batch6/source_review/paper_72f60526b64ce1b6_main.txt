Eﬀect of bridge type on electronic structure and
rectification in molecular junctions
Morad M. El-Hendawy,
*ab Hend S. Abd Elkhaira and Mahmoud M. A. Mohameda
This study links the chemistry of isolated molecules with the physics of two-probe systems by exploring
the role of molecular bridges in rectification through density functional theory (DFT) and nonequilibrium
Green’s function (DFT–NEGF) methods. Two classes of molecules are studied: Group A (A–bridge–D),
which is based on a pyrimidinyl–phenyl dithiol scaffold where the pyrimidinyl ring acts as an electron
acceptor (A) and the phenyl ring as a donor (D), with thiol anchoring groups; and Group B (2A–bridge–
2D), which uses dipyrimidinyl–diphenyl dithiol scaffolds designed to examine the impact of extended
conjugation. For both groups, three types of bridges are investigated: s-bridge, p-bridge, and direct
linkage. The computational workflow includes (i) DFT calculations to evaluate how external electric fields
influence the structural and electronic properties of isolated molecules, and (ii) DFT–NEGF simulations
of two-probe systems, where molecules are placed between gold electrodes. Electron transport
properties—such as current–voltage (I–V) characteristics, density of states (DOS), and transmission spec-
tra—are analyzed systematically. The results indicate that s-bridge molecules exhibit the strongest
rectification, with the 2A–s–2D molecule reaching the highest rectification ratio of 6.7 at 2 V. These
findings demonstrate a clear link between the chemistry of isolated molecules and the physics of mole-
cular junctions, further supported by a linear regression correlation.
1. Introduction
Molecular electronics continues the trend of miniaturization
beyond silicon’s physical limits, oﬀering new functionalities
such as low-power operation and biosystem integration.1–3
However, challenges in device integration, interface control,
and scalable manufacturing still hinder its commercial pro-
gress. One of the most fundamental electronic components is
the diode, which allows current to flow more easily in the
forward direction than in the reverse direction.
The story of molecular electronics formally began in 1974
when Ari Aviram and Mark Ratner theoretically designed a
molecular rectifier. Their design featured donor and acceptor
moieties separated by a s-bridge, resulting in an asymmetric
charge
flow
due
to
an
intramolecularly
uneven
charge
distribution.4 This pioneering model inspired experimentalists
to fabricate and examine several examples of molecular
rectifiers.5,6 Molecular rectification is governed by the principle
of asymmetry in charge transport across molecular junctions.
This asymmetry has been demonstrated in numerous studies
through strategies such as the use of distinct molecular
cores,7,8 electrodes,9–11 anchoring groups,12–14 asymmetric
environments,15 and the incorporation of spatial variations
via molecular intercalations.16–18 In this context, El-Hendawy
et al. emphasized strategies for designing novel organic mole-
cular rectifiers using computational chemistry approaches.19
The ratio or direction of rectification of molecular junctions
can be controlled by many factors such as light, temperature,20
gate potential,21–23 protonation processes,24 and others. Recent
literature demonstrates major advances in molecular rectifiers,
with high rectification ratios,25–27 improved device integration,
and a deeper understanding of rectification mechanisms.5
Strategies such as quantum interference control, environmen-
tal tuning,15 and rational molecular design are driving the field
toward practical nanoscale electronic applications.28
Despite this progress, studies that specifically elucidate the
role of the molecular bridge in controlling and rectifying charge
transport remain limited.29–31 For instance, Su et al. demon-
strated that the rectification performance of molecular diodes
is strongly influenced by the conjugation of the central bridge,
with non-conjugated bridges yielding higher rectification ratios
(RRs) due to asymmetric electron localization under bias.29
Another NEGF-DFT study30 reveals that cross-conjugated mole-
cules with donor substituents function as efficient molecular
rectifiers, exhibiting high conductance and RRs up to 10 at
0.3 V. In contrast, donor–bridge–acceptor triads incorporating
electron-withdrawing groups achieve RRs on the order of 102.

To elucidate the role of molecular bridges in electron trans-

and without bridges, incorporating either saturated or unsatu-

through ethane or ethylene bridges. The molecules were cate-

and a pyrimidinyl acceptor, and Group B, comprising a biphe-
nyl donor and a dipyrimidinyl acceptor. Thiol groups served as
anchoring moieties in all cases, as depicted in Fig. 1.
2. Computational details
The present study was conducted in two phases of calculations:
2.1
Phase 1: isolated molecule calculations
In the first phase, the influence of an external electric field on
the geometric and electronic structures of isolated molecular
systems was investigated using Gaussian 16 Rev. B.01.32
Geometry optimizations for all molecules were carried out
using the B3LYP/6-31+G(d) model chemistry.33 To simulate
the interaction with electrodes, the terminal sulphur atoms
were spatially fixed, and the molecules were subjected to an
external electric field ranging from 2 V to +2 V.34,35 To refine
the electronic energies and related properties, single-point
energy
calculations
were
performed
using

visualization tool to prepare input files and to analyze and
visualize the results.
2.2
Phase 2: two-probe system calculations
In the second phase, the non-equilibrium Green’s function
formalism
combined
with
density
functional
theory

used for constructing the molecular junctions and analyzing all
computed properties. The B3LYP-optimized molecular struc-
tures were positioned between two Au(111) electrodes to form a
two-probe system,39 as illustrated in Fig. 2. This setup included
a left electrode, a central scattering region (comprising the
molecule and four layers of gold atoms on each end), and a
right electrode.
Minimization constraints were imposed to fix the bulk
electrode atoms, while the molecule and interfacial layers were
allowed to relax, ensuring physically meaningful two-probe
transport calculations. Geometry optimization was performed
until the maximum residual force on any atom was below
0.05 eV Å1. The eﬀects of an applied electric field on electron
transport were investigated using the PBE functional,40 with
numerical convergence ensured by a tolerance of 104.
To reduce computational cost, a single-zeta polarized (SZP)
basis set was applied to Au atoms, while a double-zeta polarized
(DZP) basis set was used for all other atoms.41–43 The Brillouin
zone was sampled using a Monkhorst–Pack k-point grid of 4 
4  130, after a thorough convergence analysis. Electrostatic
potentials were computed on a real-space grid with a mesh
cutoff energy of 75 Hartree. Each semi-infinite gold electrode
was modeled using a 3  3  2 unit cell under periodic
boundary conditions. The Cartesian coordinates of all species
in both their isolated and junction configurations were
included in the SI.

3. Results and discussion
In this section, we present the results of the first and second
phase calculations on the isolated molecules and the two-probe
system, discussed in Sections 3.1 and 3.2, respectively. Finally,
Section 3.3 correlates these findings to identify the intrinsic
properties of the isolated molecules that govern rectification in
molecular junctions.
3.1.
Isolated molecular systems
3.1.1.
Molecular geometry. Geometric optimizations of the
molecules, performed both in the absence and presence of an
electric field, revealed diﬀerences in dihedral twist angles
between the donor and acceptor moieties. p-Bridged molecules
were nearly coplanar, whereas s-bridged and non-bridged
molecules exhibited dihedral twists of approximately 271 and
381,
respectively.
Consequently,
p-bridged
molecules
are
expected to facilitate current flow due to enhanced conjugation
between the moieties. In contrast, s-bridged molecules are
likely to impede charge transfer because the ethane bridge
disrupts conjugation in addition to the dihedral twist. Variation
of the applied voltage between 2 V and 2 V resulted in only
minor changes in the dihedral angles, indicating the structural
stability of the molecules under an electric field.
3.1.2.
Dipole moment. The dipole moment and rectifica-
tion behavior of single molecules are strongly governed by the
electronic asymmetry between their conjugated moieties, ana-
logous to conventional p–n semiconductor junctions. Fig. 3
presents the variation of dipole moments for Groups A and B as
a function of applied bias voltage (2 V to 2 V). As bias
increases, dipole moments decrease in both groups as the
voltage shifts from negative to positive, indicating an asym-
metric redistribution of electronic density under an external
field. Applying a negative bias across a A–bridge–D system
aligns the external electric field with the intrinsic dipole,
facilitating electron transfer from donor to acceptor as sup-
ported by the dipole moment versus applied voltage plot
(Fig. 3). This enhances charge separation and increases the
dipole moment by stabilizing the charge-separated state. In
contrast, when the bias is reversed (positive voltage), the
external field opposes the natural donor–acceptor charge trans-
fer, thereby reducing charge separation or even reversing it,

Relative energy. The fundamental equation that
describes the interaction between an electric dipole and an

where p is the dipole moment, E is the magnitude of the electric

U = pE
(5)
The relative energy values (in eV) shown on the y-axis in Fig. 4
correspond to the potential energy of the molecule under these
conditions. The reference point (U = 0) is defined as the energy
of the A–D molecule in the absence of the applied field. The
negative sign in the above equation indicates an inverse rela-
tionship between the relative energy and the dipole moment, as
is evident in the current study by comparing Fig. 3 and 4.
The most notable result is the asymmetric response of the
molecule’s potential energy to the direction of the applied
electric field. Fig. 4 demonstrates that the change in energy
as the bias voltage shifts from negative to positive does not
follow a simple symmetric trend. This asymmetry in the
energy–bias relationship directly reflects the underlying elec-
tronic structure of the molecule and forms the physical basis of
its rectifying behavior. The difference in energy states between
positive and negative bias voltages indicates that the potential
barrier for electron transport is also asymmetric. Consequently,
electrons moving in one direction experience a lower barrier,
leading to enhanced conductivity, whereas in the reverse direc-
tion, they face a higher barrier, resulting in current suppres-
sion. Thus, the observed energy asymmetry provides strong
evidence that these molecules can function as molecular
rectifiers.
Additionally, changes in the applied voltage can give rise to
new molecular conformations because of modifications in their
electronic structures. This eﬀect is evident in the steady
increase of relative energy observed when moving from negative
to positive bias, as illustrated in Fig. 4.

Comparing the HOMO–LUMO energy gap (Eg) of the groups
(A) and (B) molecules, it’s evident that doubling the donor–
acceptor units leads to a lower Eg for all molecule types in

and bringing them closer in energy. On the other hand, the Eg
of all molecules, especially the s-bridged ones, changes more
dramatically and rapidly under negative bias than under posi-
tive bias. s-Bridged molecules show the strongest asymmetry
between the two regions (positive and negative bias). This
pronounced difference in Eg behaviour means that the mole-
cule becomes significantly more conductive under negative
bias than under positive bias.34 This strong asymmetry in
conductivity directly leads to current rectification. When the
molecule is connected in a circuit, a negative voltage will result
in a much higher current flow (due to the smaller Eg) compared
to a positive voltage of the same magnitude (due to the
larger Eg), effectively acting as a molecular diode.
Applying an external voltage to a molecule changes the
electron distribution in its FMOs. This change aﬀects the
energy gap between these orbitals and their alignment with
the electrodes, which is a critical factor in determining how
well the molecule conducts electricity and thus reflects their
potential suitability as molecular diodes. The s-bridged mole-

Fig. 7. Upon applying a negative bias voltage, the HOMO and
LUMO remain distinctly localized on the donor and acceptor
moieties, respectively. In contrast, reversing the bias to a
positive voltage causes this localization to reverse, with the
electron density shifting towards the opposite ends of the
molecule. This localization is a key feature of an Aviram–Ratner
rectifier.4 A similar, albeit mixed, behavior was observed in the
non-bridged molecules. This asymmetric response to the elec-
tric field confirms that both s-bridged and non-bridged sys-
tems
possess
the

to
function

From a preliminary observation, the A–p–D molecule functions
as a molecular wire rather than a diode. Its HOMO and LUMO are
highly delocalized across the entire molecule. This symmetrical
and stable electron distribution persists even when the bias
voltage is reversed from negative to positive. Such consistent
delocalization enables electrons to move freely and efficiently in
both directions, which is the defining characteristic of a molecu-
lar wire.34 However, upon duplicating the phenyl and pyrimidyl
rings, 2A–p–2D, the strength of the donor and acceptor increases,
leading to an observed shift in the electron density of the HOMO
and LUMO toward the donor and acceptor moieties, respectively,
upon applying a negative voltage. This behaviour is reversed when
a positive bias is applied. The findings further emphasize the
influence of molecular size on electronic properties.
3.2.
Two-probe systems
In this section, the physics of molecular function will be
addressed. In this regard, the I–V characteristics, density of
states (DOS), transmission spectra, and other related aspects
were discussed.
3.2.1.
Analysis of molecular rectification and current–
voltage characteristics. The current–voltage (I–V) characteristics
and RR are key parameters for evaluating molecular junction
performance. The RR (RR = |IV+/IV|), defined as the forward-to-
reverse current ratio at equal bias, quantifies current asymme-
try, with larger values indicating stronger rectification. In
molecular junctions, electron transport proceeds from the
donor to the acceptor under positive bias, resulting in current
flow from the acceptor to the donor.49,50
Charge transport weakens with increasing molecular length,
consistent with the findings of Huang et al.,47 where shorter
Group A junctions exhibit higher currents than their longer
Group B counterparts. For instance, at 2.00 V, the current
decreases from E9470 nA in A–D to E3519 nA in 2A–2D,
from E490 nA in A–s–D to E241 nA in 2A–s–2D, and from
E16 341 nA in A–p–D to E7006 nA in 2A–p–2D (Fig. 8). This
reduction reflects weaker electronic coupling to the electrodes.
The nature of the bridge further modulates coupling. p-
Conjugated bridges, with delocalized electrons, provide stron-
ger coupling and higher current, whereas s-bridges, lacking
delocalization, result in weaker transport. Within each group,
p-bridged molecules (A–p–D, 2A–p–2D) exhibit the highest
currents, followed by non-bridged systems (A–D, 2A–2D), while
s-bridged molecules (A–s–D, 2A–s–2D) show the lowest. The
apparent constant current of A–s–D and 2A–s–2D in Fig. 8 is
only due to the plot scale. The I–V curves also display asym-
metry around zero bias, a hallmark of rectifying behavior.
s-Bridged junctions exhibit the strongest asymmetry and
highest RRs relative to p-bridged and non-bridged systems. In
Group A, RRs at 1 V follow A–s–D (E1.4) 4 A–p–D (E1.3) 4 A–

3.2.2
Projected density of states (PDOS) spectra of terminal
sulfur atoms. The density of states (DOS) is a fundamental
property for understanding electron transport in molecular
junctions. It quantifies the number of available electronic
states per unit energy that electrons can occupy.53 A high
DOS at a particular energy level signifies a high density of
available states, which facilitates more eﬃcient electron flow.
Conversely, a low DOS indicates fewer states, impeding electron
transport. However, the projected density of states (PDOS)
provides insight into the electronic coupling between specific
atoms and their surroundings. As shown in Fig. 9, peaks in the
PDOS of the sulfur anchor atoms (S1 and S2) near the Fermi
level (E  EF = 0) indicate strong electronic coupling with the
gold electrodes. This strong coupling enhances electron trans-
fer eﬃciency across the junction. At zero bias, the PDOS of both
S1 and S2 appears nearly identical, suggesting symmetrical
coupling between the molecule and the electrodes. While at
negative bias (1 V), the PDOS of S1 (left sulfur atom) shifts
upward, moving closer to the Fermi level, while the PDOS of S2
(right sulfur atom) shifts below the Fermi level. This indicates
that the S2 states are becoming more occupied with electrons
and more localized, reflecting the chemical potential shift
associated with electron flow from left to right. On the other
hand, at 1 V, the opposite behaviour is observed. The PDOS of
S2 shifts upward toward the Fermi level, while the PDOS of S1
shifts downward. This suggests a potential for electron flow
from right to left.
Upon comparing the intensity of the PDOS peaks near the
Fermi level, it is evident that the intensity for S2 at +1 V is
stronger than the intensity for S1 at 1 V. This indicates that
while electrons can move in both directions, the molecule
exhibits a preference for right-to-left electron movement. This
preference is attributed to the higher abundance of PDOS states
for S2, which makes it more susceptible to electron injection
from the right electrode and thereby demonstrates the mole-
cule’s rectifying ability.

4. Conclusions
Six
donor–bridge–acceptor
molecular
systems
with
thiol
anchoring groups were investigated using NEGF-DFT formal-
ism. The study examined the influence of bridge type (s-bridge,
p-bridge, or no bridge) and molecular length (single vs. doubled
donor and acceptor units) on electronic structure, transport
properties, and rectification performance under applied bias.
The key findings are summarized as follows:
1. Rectification strongly depends on bridge type, following
the trend: s-bridge 4 no bridge 4 p-bridge. The saturated
s-bridge restricts donor–acceptor coupling, favouring asymme-
try, whereas p-bridges enhance donor–acceptor communica-
tion, reducing rectification.
2. Group B molecules exhibit higher RRs than Group A because
the greater number of nitrogen atoms in the acceptor moiety
enhances asymmetric electron transport, improving rectification.
3. The smaller Group A molecules, particularly A–p–D and A–
D, show rectification ratios close to unity, suggesting largely
symmetric current flow across the examined bias window, a
characteristic behaviour of molecular wires.
4. The 2A–s–2D device exhibited the highest rectification
ratio (6.7 at 2 V), though its coupling with electrodes remained
limited.
5. The influence of molecular signatures on rectification
behavior was confirmed through the mathematical correlation
