A B S T R A C T
Conjugated polymers (CPs), with the features of tailorable molecular structure and property, are an emerging 
class of photocatalytic materials for resolving global energy and environmental crises. However, due to the 
strong exciton effect and low charge carrier separation efficiency, the photogenerated charge utilization effi­
ciency in CPs-based photocatalysts is poor, and the realization of satisfactory photocatalytic redox reaction 
performance remains a difficult subject. Herein, based on molecular design principles, we propose a novel edge 
activation strategy to construct a series of donor-acceptor (D-A) type linear conjugated polymers (LCPs). Edge- 
activated LCPs exhibit good electron delocalization, allowing photogenerated electrons to migrate along the 
main chain and be directly transferred to the edge of the polymer chain. Experiments combined with theoretical 
calculations indicate that this design strategy can effectively reduce exciton binding energy to 33 meV, promote 
charge directional migration, inhibit charge recombination, and enable more photogenerated charges to 
participate in the photocatalytic redox reactions. In particular, the optimal sample achieved the apparent 
quantum yield of 20.9% at 420 nm for the photocatalytic H2 evolution reaction. The present result of this work 
will provide a new design pathway for preparing CPs-based photocatalysts with high charge separation efficiency 
and excellent performance for light-driven redox reactions.
1. Introduction
Utilizing photocatalytic technology to convert solar energy into 
chemical energy stands as a highly viable solution to tackle global en­
ergy and environmental crises [1,2]. However, the current efficiency of 
photocatalytic technology in converting solar energy to chemical energy 
is inadequate for industrial applications. Therefore, it is imperative to 
develop affordable and highly efficient photocatalysts swiftly. Over the 
past few decades, inorganic semiconductor photocatalysts, such as metal 
sulfides and metal oxides, have been the main focus of extensive 
research [3,4]. In recent years, conjugated polymers (CPs) based pho­
tocatalysts have gained attention by virtue of their molecularly tailor­
able structures and naturally abundant element composition [5–7]. Up 
to now, a variety of CPs have been explored and used for photocatalytic 
reactions, including carbon nitride [8–11], covalent triazine frameworks 
(CTFs) [12–15], covalent organic frameworks (COFs) [16–19], linear 
conjugated polymers (LCPs) [20–22], porous organic polymers (POPs) 
[23–26], and so on. However, due to the strong exciton (bound state of 
electron−hole pairs) effect and limited charge carrier mobility of CPs- 
based photocatalysts, the charge separation efficiency is insufficient, 
which prevents most photogenerated charges from effectively partici­
pating in the photocatalytic reaction, thus resulting in an unsatisfactory 
performance [27–29].
The most popular molecular design tactic to enhance the efficiency of 
photogenerated charge separation is to assemble a donor-acceptor (D-A) 
system using electron-deficient donor units and electron-rich acceptor 
units [30,31]. In the D-A type CPs-based photocatalysts, the polarization 
between the donor and acceptor leads to the migration of

arrangement causes photogenerated electrons to be localized in the 
acceptor unit rather than migrating along the polymer backbone, thus 
impeding the intrachain transport of photogenerated charges [33]. 
Therefore, to mitigate the adverse effects, an edge activation strategy is 
proposed herein for designing D-A CPs (Scheme 1b). This edge activa­
tion strategy is realized by introducing electron-withdrawing sub­
stituents at the edges of the electron-rich polymer backbone. 
Specifically, this strategy develops a class of highly polarized building 
blocks, in which electron-rich and electron-deficient moieties are stra­
tegically positioned at the opposite ends of the molecule to construct the 
D-A system linked by π-linkers. Firstly, this configuration enables elec­
tron delocalization along the chain and allows the migration of photo­
generated electrons along the polymer backbone. More importantly, it 
induces appropriate spontaneous polarization along the polymer back­
bone, which may trigger directional charge migration between the 
opposing edges of the polymer chain, thereby synergistically promoting 
the spatial separation of photogenerated charges. On this basis, by 
further imparting the capability to output charges, the edge of the 
polymer would be activated as a crucial delivery channel, facilitating the 
completion of directional charge migration toward the active sites.
Herein, we performed a proof-of-concept study on the effect of edge 
activation strategy. Firstly, desired building blocks with different dipole 
moments were designed by introducing 0 to 2 cyano groups at the 3−/4- 
position of the thiophene molecule. Then, these thiophene-based 
building blocks were integrated with benzene rings to design a series 
of edge-activated LCPs (Scheme 1c). With the introduction of cyano 
groups at the para-position of the electron-rich S atom, a highly polar­
ized building block is formed, augmenting the dipole moments of the 
individual moieties and the entire LCPs system. Additionally, the cyano 
group could further function as a potential electron-output channel to 
the active sites. The combination of experimental and theoretical find­
ings of LCPs has provided critical evidence supporting our design 
strategy aiming at boosting exciton dissociation and charge separation. 
Expectedly, the optimal LCP with a large dipole moment (L-Th-2CN) 
demonstrated outstanding photocatalytic activities in a series of 
important photocatalytic redox reactions (H2 evolution, O2 evolution, 
and H2O2 production reactions). Our work provides an innovative 
perspective for designing the molecular structure of CPs-based 
photocatalysts.

2.2. Photocatalytic performance
2.2.1. Photocatalytic H2 evolution test
Photocatalytic hydrogen evolution arrays were performed in a Pyrex 
top-irradiation reaction vessel linked to a glass closed gas system. H2 
production was carried out by dispersing 10 mg of photocatalyst powder 
in an aqueous solution (110 mL) containing triethanolamine (10 mL) as 
the sacrificial electron donor. 9.0 wt% of Pt was loaded on the surface of 
the photocatalyst by the in-situ photodeposition approach using 
H2PtCl6. The mixture was evacuated several times to remove air 
completely before irradiation under a 300 W Xe-lamp. The wavelength 
of the incident light was controlled by using a long-pass cut-off filter 
>420 nm (or other appropriate long-pass cut-off filters) for testing the 
wavelength-dependent hydrogen evolution rate of the sample. The 
temperature of the reaction solution was kept at 12 ◦C by a flow of 
cooling water. The generated gases were analyzed by gas chromatog­
raphy equipped with a thermal conductive detector (TCD) with Argon as 
the carrier gas.
2.2.2. Photocatalytic O2 evolution test
Photocatalytic O2 production was measured in a Pyrex top- 
irradiation reaction vessel connected to a glass closed gas circulation 
system. For each reaction, 10 mg catalyst powder was well dispersed in 
an aqueous solution (100 mL) containing AgNO3 (0.01 M) as an electron 
acceptor and La2O3 (0.2 g) as a pH buffer agent. The reactant solution 
was evacuated several times to completely remove air before irradiation 
under a 300 W Xe lamp and a water-cooling filter. The temperature of 
the reactant solution was maintained at 12 ◦C by a flow of cooling water 
during the reaction. The evolved gases were analyzed by gas chroma­
tography equipped with a thermal conductive detector (TCD).
2.2.3. Photocatalytic H2O2 production test
The photocatalytic reactions of hydrogen peroxide evolution were 
carried out in a Schlenk flask. In the Schlenk flask, 10 mg of catalyst 
powder was dispersed in 20 mL of deionized water by ultrasonication for 
15 min. Then, O2 was bubbled through the solution for 15 min. Flowing 
water was employed to control the reaction temperature at 298 K. Then, 
the system was irradiated with a 50 W LED lamp (λ = 420 nm). After the 
reaction, the H2O2 amount was determined using a Shimadzu UV-1780 
UV–Vis spectrophotometer by measuring its absorbance based on the 
formation of the yellow complex TiIV-H2O2. When titanium oxysulfate 
(TiOSO4) reacted with H2O2, a yellow complex (pertitanic acid) was 
formed, and UV −
Vis measurement was done at 410 nm to colorimet­
rically determine the concentration of H2O2 (TiO2+ +H2O2 → [TiO 
(H2O2)]2+).
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Synthesis and characterization of LCPs
In this work, three LCPs were synthesized by Pd(0)-catalyzed Suzuki- 
Miyaura cross-coupling reaction consisting of the same π linker building 
block (1,4-phenylenediboronic acid) and different edge-activated 
building block (2,5-dibromothiophene, 2,5-dibromo-3-cyanothiophene, 
and 2,5-dibromo-3,4-dicyanothiophene).
Based on the fact that the obtained LCPs have different amounts of 
cyano groups, they are named L-Th-0CN, L-Th-1CN, and L-Th-2CN, 
respectively. All the resulting LCPs were insoluble in both water and 
organic solvents. The powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) pattern indicated 
the semi-crystalline nature of all the LCPs (Fig. S2) [34]. The PXRD 
pattern of L-Th-0CN is consistent with the previous report [35]. 
Furthermore, it can be observed that as the number of cyano groups 
increases, the crystallinity of LCPs exhibits a trend of first decreasing and 
then increasing, while the packing distance shows an opposite trend of 
first increasing and then decreasing. Scanning electron microscopy 
(SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were utilized to 
observe the morphology of LCPs (Fig. 1a, Figs. S3 and S4). Meanwhile, 
the energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) element mapping images of 
LCPs show the homogeneous distribution of various elements (Fig. 1a 
and Fig. S4). The porosity of the LCPs was measured by N2 adsorption/ 
desorption isotherms at 77 K. As shown in Fig. S5, the specific surface 
areas of L-Th-0CN, L-Th-1CN, and L-Th-2CN are 26, 75, and 62 m2 g−1, 
respectively. The low surface area phenomenon has also been observed 
in other similar 1D linear polymer systems because of their condensed 
morphology [36]. Furthermore, due to the addition of the Pd catalyst in 
the synthesis process of LCPs, there is inevitably Pd residue in LCPs, 
which is measured by inductively coupled plasma mass spectra (ICP-MS) 
and shown in Table S1.
To confirm the successful synthesis of LCPs, the Fourier transform 
infrared (FT-IR) spectra were first obtained (Fig. 1b). Peaks located at 
1603 and 689 cm−1 can be assigned to stretching vibrations of aromatic 
C––C and C–S bonds, respectively [37]. Notably, a strong characteristic 
signal at around 2220 cm−1 originates from the cyano groups in the 
thiophene ring [38]. The 13C cross-polarization magic angle spin (CP- 
MAS) solid-state NMR spectra were also performed to obtain more 
detailed information regarding the characteristic structure of LCPs. As 
shown in Fig. 1c, the prominent peaks at around 130 and 113 ppm are 
attributed to the carbons of the benzene ring and cyano groups, 
respectively [39]. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was 
employed to further investigate the surface chemical composition of 
LCPs (Fig. 1d-f). In the high-resolution C 1 s XPS spectra of LCPs, the 
peak at 284.6 eV belongs to the aromatic C––C species. Compared with 
L-Th-0CN, the high-resolution C 1 s XPS spectra of L-Th-1CN and L-Th- 
2CN showed an extra peak at around 285.9 eV corresponding to C–––N in 
cyano groups. Moreover, the increased C–––N peak ratio of the high- 
resolution C 1 s XPS spectra corroborates the highest number of cyano 
groups in L-Th-2CN. In the high-resolution N 1 s XPS spectra, L-Th-1CN 
and L-Th-2CN display one peak at around 398.9 eV, ascribed to the N 
atoms of cyano groups [40]. The high-resolution S 2p XPS spectra can be 
resolved into two peaks, which are assigned to S(II) species in the 
thiophene ring [41]. Especially, the peaks corresponding to S atoms shift 
to higher binding energy with the increase of the number of cyano 
groups in the thiophene ring. This phenomenon is due to the strong 
electron-withdrawing ability of the introduced cyano groups, which can 
effectively reduce the electron density of S atoms [42].
The light-harvesting capacity and band structure of LCPs were 
analyzed using ultraviolet-visible diffuse reflectance spectra (UV–vis 
DRS) and Mott-Schottky (M-S) curves. As displayed in Fig. 2a, all the 
LCPs exhibit broad absorption in the UV to visible light region, 
demonstrating their excellent light-harvesting capability. The absorp­
tion edges of L-Th-0CN, L-Th-1CN, and L-Th-2CN are at 533, 569, and 
522 nm, respectively, which is in good agreement with the observed 
color of the LCPs. According to the absorption edge values of LCPs, the 
band gaps (Eg) of L-Th-0CN, L-Th-1CN, and L-Th-2CN were calculated to 
be 2.33, 2.18, and 2.38 eV, respectively. Subsequently, the M-S curves 
were obtained to calculate the flat band positions of LCPs, which are 
approximately equal to the conduction band (CB) positions. As shown in 
Fig. S6, all the M-S curves of LCPs exhibit a positive slope, which il­
lustrates their n-type semiconductor nature. The CB positions of L-Th- 
0CN, L-Th-1CN, and L-Th-2CN, derived from the intersection, are −0.46,

Fig. 2e shows the steady-state PL spectra of LCPs excited at 360 nm. 
From L-Th-0CN to L-Th-1CN and then to L-Th-2CN, the emission peak 
intensity decreases gradually, indicating efficient suppression of 
recombination of photogenerated charges. Time-correlated single-

the average PL lifetimes, which were found to be 1.74 ns for L-Th-0CN, 
1.81 ns for L-Th-1CN, and 2.24 ns for L-Th-2CN, respectively (Fig. S8). 
The prolonged lifetime of L-Th-2CN signifies suppressed excited-state 
charge recombination and more efficient charge separation. Subse­
quently, steady-state surface photovoltage (SPV) spectra were employed 
to further evaluate the charge migration efficiency of the LCPs (Fig. 2f 
and Fig. S9). L-Th-2CN exhibited the strongest SPV intensity relative to 
L-Th-0CN and L-Th-1CN, indicative of enhanced photogenerated charge 
separation and transport efficiency. The Zeta potential result of LCPs 
also verifies that L-Th-2CN has a larger surface charge density than 
others (Fig. S10) [46].
Further density functional theory (DFT) calculations were carried 
out to help us understand the effect of molecular structure adjustment on 
electron distribution and dipole field in LCPs. By first analyzing the 
molecular electrostatic potential (MESP) maps of three monomers, it can 
be found that the positive charge is concentrated on the thiophene ring, 
and the negative charge was concentrated on the cyano group, which 
form obvious electron-rich and electron-deficient sectors at opposite 
ends of the molecule, indicating the successful construction of the edge- 
activated D-A system building block (Fig. S11). As the number of cyano 
groups in the monomer increases, the asymmetry of the electron-rich 
and electron-deficient sectors in the D-A system improves, resulting in 
the monomer possessing a larger dipole moment. The dipole moment 
can generate a dipole field in LCPs, induce the directional transfer of 
photogenerated charges, and promote charge separation. Interestingly, 
the introduction of highly polarized D-A monomers as building blocks in 
LCPs results in not only a significant spatial separation of the positive 
and negative charge distributions but also a further increase in the 
dipole moment of the polymer compared to that of the monomer 
(Fig. 3a). Notably, the MESP potential difference of the LCPs increases 
significantly with the number of cyano groups, rising from 19.86 kcal 
mol−1 for L-Th-0CN to 45.79 kcal mol−1 for L-Th-1CN and further to 
50.10 kcal mol−1 for L-Th-2CN. In addition, the highest occupied mo­
lecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital 
(LUMO) of the three LCPs were calculated and displayed in Fig. S12. 
Compared with L-Th-0CN and L-Th-1CN, L-Th-2CN has a more pro­
nounced separation of HOMO and LUMO distributions, while still 
maintaining a longer range of electron delocalization along the polymer 
main chain to support the subsequent migration of charges.
In-situ XPS analysis was used to monitor photogenerated charge 
transfer paths in L-Th-0CN and L-Th-2CN after photoexcitation. In 
general, the movement of a chemical element toward higher binding 
energy in the XPS spectra is due to the increased electron cloud density, 
indicating the transfer of electrons to this element [47,48]. For L-Th- 
0CN, the binding energies of the C 1 s and S 2p peaks barely changed 
before and after photoexcitation, indicating weak electron transfer 
(Fig. S13). For L-Th-2CN, the binding energy of S 2p peaks proceeds a 
negative shift of about 0.12 eV, and that of N 1 s peak proceeds a positive 
shift of about 0.14 eV after photoexcitation, indicating that the photo­
generated electrons transfer directionally from S atoms to N atoms 
(Fig. 3b-d). Especially, previous reports have verified that cyano groups 
can be further used as an electron delivery channel [38,43,49], thus 
allowing the outputting of concentrated electrons to the active sites to 
drive the photocatalytic reaction. The above results confirm the feasi­
bility of the edge activation strategy.

3.2. Photocatalytic performance of LCPs
The activity of the photocatalytic H2 evolution reaction was first 
measured to assess the photocatalytic ability of LCPs under visible light 
irradiation, using Pt as the cocatalyst. After adjusting the additional 
amounts of Pt cocatalyst and photocatalyst (Fig. S15 and S16), L-Th- 
2CN achieved an outstanding photocatalytic H2 evolution rate of 287 
μmol h−1, with 10 mg of L-Th-2CN and 9 wt% of Pt cocatalyst. Under the 
optimized conditions, the photocatalytic H2 production amount of three 
LCPs increased in a near-linear trend (Fig. S17). Remarkably, as shown 
in Fig. 4a, the photocatalytic H2 evolution rate of L-Th-2CN is 143.5 
times that of L-Th-0CN and 1.6 times that of L-Th-1CN. The AQY is 
considered a reliable criterion for evaluating photocatalysts in different 
systems. Thus, the AQY of L-Th-2CN was determined to be 20.5%, 
20.9%, 19.7%, 8.8%, and 0.3% at wavelengths of 380, 420, 450, 500, 
and 600 nm, respectively, consistent with the trend of the UV–vis DRS 
(Fig. 4b). It is worth mentioning that the photocatalytic H2 evolution 
rate and AQY at 420 nm of L-Th-2CN are superior to those of most re­
ported conjugated polymeric semiconductors (Fig. 4c and Table S3). 
During the 20-h-long-term photocatalytic test, L-Th-2CN maintained a 
stable hydrogen evolution amount, demonstrating its excellent photo­
stability under continuous light irradiation (Fig. 4d). Meanwhile, the 
structural integrity and morphological stability of LCPs showed no sig­
nificant difference before and after the photocatalytic H2 evolution re­
action, as supported by FT-IR spectra, XRD patterns, and TEM images 
(Fig. S18-S21). Further PL measurements were performed to assess 
photogenerated electron transfer and interactions between LCPs and 
active sites (Pt co-catalysts). After Pt loading, the PL intensities of all 
LCPs decreased to varying degrees, and L-Th-2CN exhibited the most 
significant PL quenching, verifying the electron-output capability of 
cyano groups (Fig. S22).
The oxygen evolution reaction involving a four-electron transfer 
process is still a challenge owing to the large overpotential and slow 
kinetics [7]. In theory, the band positions of LCPs satisfy the re­
quirements of the water oxidation potential. Therefore, the photo­
catalytic O2 evolution rate of three LCPs was tested by using AgNO3 as 
the sacrificial electron acceptor and Co species as the cocatalyst under 
UV/vis light irradiation (λ > 300 nm). We first determined that the 
optimal amount of Co cocatalyst added is 2 wt% (Fig. S23). Subse­
quently, the photocatalytic O2 evolution rate of L-Th-0CN, L-Th-1CN, 
and L-Th-2CN was measured to be 0.4, 4.8, and 10.2 μmol h−1, 
respectively, when the amount of Co addition is 2 wt% (Fig. 4e). To the 
best of our knowledge, the photocatalytic O2 evolution rate of L-Th-2CN 
is high level compared to most reported conjugated polymers 
(Table S4). Furthermore, the time-dependent curve of the photo­
catalytic O2 evolution reaction of L-Th-2CN is displayed in Fig. S24. The 
O2 evolution rate of L-Th-2CN decreased gradually with time, which is 
due to the deposition of Ag particles on the surface of L-Th-2CN.

LCPs. As an important chemical, H2O2 has a wide range of applications 
in medicine and chemical production [50]. Currently, the photocatalytic 
H2O2 production reaction can offer a green and sustainable means of 
replacing the traditional anthraquinone process. The photocatalytic 
H2O2 generation performance of LCPs was measured under 420 nm LED 
lamp irradiation in O2-saturated water without adding any additional 
sacrificial agent. As shown in Fig. 4f, compared with L-Th-0CN (7 μmol) 
and L-Th-1CN (23 μmol) under the same conditions, the H2O2 generated

4. Conclusion
In summary, this work proposed a new edge activation strategy to 
promote the photogenerated charge separation efficiency of CPs-based 
photocatalysts by introducing large polarity D-A-type building blocks 
with electron-rich and electron-poor sectors located at opposite ends of 
the molecule into the LCPs skeleton. To validate this edge activation 
strategy, we designed a series of D-A-type LCPs by varying the number of 
cyano groups. The experimental and theoretical results demonstrated 
that the edge activation strategy can induce appropriate polarization on 
the polymer main chain to reduce electron localization and make the 
photogenerated electrons migrate to the edge of the LCPs along the main 
chain, thus improving the charge utilization efficiency. As a typical 
photocatalyst, L-Th-2CN, with the highest cyano group content, 
exhibited a low exciton binding energy (33 meV) and excellent charge 
carrier transfer efficiency. Therefore, it achieved surprising activity in 
photocatalytic redox reactions (H2 evolution, O2 evolution, and H2O2 
production reaction), which was superior to most previously reported 
polymeric photocatalysts. Especially for the photocatalytic H2 evolution 
reaction, the apparent quantum yield of L-Th-2CN at 420 nm reached 
20.9%. Our study confirmed that the edge activation strategy can ach­
ieve the directional transfer of photogenerated electrons and enhance 
the utilization efficiency of photogenerated charges, offering a novel 
perspective for the molecular design of D-A-type CPs-based 
photocatalysts.
