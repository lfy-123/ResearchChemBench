ABSTRACT 
The rapid rise of antimicrobial resistance necessitates the development of new chemotypes with improved efficacy and drug- 
like properties. In this study, 10 previously unexplored azetidine–pyridazine scaffolds were synthesized under mild, metal-free 
conditions, and evaluated for their dual antibacterial and antifungal potential. Several derivatives displayed strong in vitro 
activity against Staphylococcus aureus , with five compounds outperforming chloramphenicol. Two compounds also exhibited 
twofold higher antifungal potency than griseofulvin against Candida albicans . All molecules demonstrated favourable ADMET 
characteristics, including high predicted gastrointestinal absorption, no violations of drug-likeness rules, and non-substrate 
behaviour toward P-glycoprotein. DFT-based electronic analysis and molecular docking against DNA gyrase (5MMN) and CYP51 
(3JUV) provided molecular-level justification for the observed SAR and antimicrobial effects. Overall, the combination of green 
synthesis, potent bioactivity, desirable pharmacokinetics, and strong target interactions highlights azetidine–pyridazine scaffolds 
as promising leads for future antimicrobial drug development.

Introduction 
The worldwide surge in antimicrobial resistance (AMR) contin- 
ues to erode the effectiveness of existing drugs, posing an impend- 
ing threat to public health and the viability of contemporary 
medicines. In accordance with the World Health Organization 
(WHO), bacterial and fungal infections resistant to conventional 
drugs are causing an increasing number of disorders and deaths, 
underscoring an urgent need for novel pharmaceuticals with dis- 
tinctive structural backbones and modes of action [ 1 ]. Literature 
suggests that nitrogen-containing heterocycles are appreciated 
scaffolds in medicinal chemistry, offering a foundation for a 
broad range of valuable antibacterial and antifungal supplements 
[ 2 ]. Particularly, linezolid, an oxazolidinone-class antibiotic, and 
fluconazole, a triazole antifungal, exemplify how heterocyclic 
frameworks promote bioactivity through hydrogen bonding, π–π
stacking, and interactions with microbial enzymes or ribosomal 
targets [ 3, 4 ]. While such scaffolds exhibit long-term efficacy,

Azetidine-3-carboxamide derivatives have recently emerged as 
affirming therapeutics due to their rigid, strained four-membered 
ring architecture [ 5 ]. The inflexibility and structural constraints 
imposed by the azetidine ring promote receptor-binding selec- 
tivity, pharmacokinetic features, and metabolic stability [ 6 ]. 
Furthermore, the amide linkage offers distinct possibilities for 
hydrogen bonding, resulting in robust intermolecular interac- 
tions with microbial targets [ 7 ]. Despite these advantages, the 
effective use of azetidine-3-carboxamide derivatives in a dual 
antimicrobial (antibacterial and antifungal) context remains 
underexplored, particularly when accompanied by a variety of 
heterocycles, such as substituted pyridines, phenyl groups, and 
piperazines. 
According to recent findings, hybrid molecules that incorporate 
azetidine rings with nitrogen-rich aromatic heterocycles exhibit 
optimized activity profiles due to their synergistic structural 
features [ 8–10 ]. Interestingly, a literature survey suggests consid- 
erable gap in the rational design and demanding evaluation of 
such hybridized frameworks that adopt both experimental and 
computational approaches. Notably, a few studies focus solely on 
in vitro data without establishing bioactivity, adopting density 
functional theory (DFT) or molecular docking, which may help 
anticipate and rationalize electronic behavior, binding affinity, 
and conformational reactivity [ 11 ]. 
Considering the aforementioned, we proposed and synthesized a 
novel azetidine-3-carboxamide derivative AZ1 - AZ10 , 1-(2-ben- 
zyl-6-chloro-3-oxo-2,3-dihydropyridazin-4-yl)- N -(aryl)azetidine- 
3-carboxamide, which is comprised of an N -benzyl substituted 
pyridazine-3-carboxamide moiety, and a carboxamide-linked 
azetidine ring (Figure 1 ). The inclusion of pyridazine enhances 
membrane permeability and bioavailability, while the aryl and 
heteroaryl substituents promote hydrophobic interactions at the 
target site [ 12 ]. The pharmacophores drove the above strategy, 
as observed in defloxacin, azelinidipine, relugolix, ponatinib, 
linezolid, and fluconazole, etc., although it was further modified 
to prevent the development of resistance mechanisms [ 13, 14 ]. 
The compounds were comprehensively examined for antibacte- 
rial and antifungal activity, and they exhibit significant potential 
against a wide range of Gram-positive, Gram-negative, and fungal 
microbes. DFT simulations were employed to evaluate frontier 
molecular orbitals (HOMO-LUMO gap), molecular electrostatic 
potential (MEP), and molecular stability, providing biologi- 
cal insights through electronic and structural features. These 
quantum-chemical descriptors offered insights into the com- 
pounds’ reactivity and potential biological interactions. More- 
over, a molecular docking study with bacterial DNA gyrase and 
fungal lanosterol 14 α-demethylase (CYP51) revealed favorable 
binding affinities and key interactions similar to those observed 
with conventional drugs. Incorporating both computational and 
experimental techniques, molecular design and its structural- 
activity correlations (SARs) were emphasized to guide subse- 
quent modifications. This work accordingly presents a novel 
scaffold exhibiting antimicrobial potential, a finding supported by 
computational validation.

The novelty of this work lies in the strategic fusion of azeti- 
dine and pyridazine pharmacophores, along with the deliberate 
incorporation of electron-withdrawing (F, Cl) and hydrophobic 
(CH3 ) substituents to modulate steric, electronic, and biological 
properties. To establish the therapeutic relevance of these new 
molecules, the objectives of the study were to synthesize and char- 
acterize the derivatives, evaluate their in vitro antibacterial and 
antifungal activities, analyze structure–activity relationships, and 
employ molecular docking and DFT calculations to understand 
their binding behavior and electronic nature. Additionally, in sil- 
ico ADMET profiling was performed to assess their drug-likeness 
and pharmacokinetic suitability. Together, these objectives aim 
to identify promising azetidine–pyridazine based candidates for 
future development as dual-action antimicrobial agents.

Results and Discussion 
2.1 
Synthesis 
10 novel azetidine-pyridazine linked scaffolds 1-(2-ben- 
zyl-6-chloro-3-oxo-2,3-dihydropyridazin-4-yl)- N -(aryl)azetidine- 
3-carboxamide AZ1-AZ10 bearing various functionalities 
were synthesized. Primarily, 3,4,6-trichloropyridazine 1 was 
refluxed in the presence of acetic acid to give 4,6-dichloro-2 H - 
pyridazin-3-one 2 , which was then treated with benzyl bromide 
in the presence of potassium carbonate and DMF at room 
temperature to produce 2-benzyl-4,6-dichloropyridazin-3(2 H )- 
one 3 . Furthermore, protected amide intermediates 5a-5j were 
prepared from azetidine-3-carboxylic acid derivative 4 using 
corresponding amines, incorporating HATU and DIPEA in 
DMF solvent at room temperature. These intermediates were 
then treated with TFA in DCM solvent at ambient temperature, 
yielding the deprotected amines 6a-6j . These compounds, 
obtained as 2-benzyl-4,6-dichloropyridazin-3(2H)-one 3 and 
deprotected amines 6a-6j , were then reacted with the DIPEA 
reagent in DMF solvent at room temperature, yielding AZ1- 
AZ10 . The synthetic methodology is proposed in Scheme 1 . The 
structural integrity of the synthesized compounds was confirmed 
through comprehensive spectroscopic analysis, including NMR, 
mass spectrometry, and IR spectroscopy [ 15–18 ].

DNA gyrase (5MMN) and CYP51 (3JUV). Compounds displaying

reflected by their highly negative MolDock and Re-rank scores.

polar anchoring and deep hydrophobic insertion stabilizes lig- 
and orientation and enhances inhibitory potential. Conversely, 
compounds showing comparatively weaker antimicrobial activity 
possessed higher (less favorable) docking scores and reduced 
interaction density, indicating a diminished ability to engage key 
residues within the gyrase binding pocket. A similar correlation

and position influence how easily the compound can interact 
with or penetrate microbial cells. Compounds with appropriately 
sized substituents, large enough to enhance membrane interac-

better antibacterial activity, as seen with AZ5 , AZ7 , and AZ8 . 
When substituents are positioned at locations that maintain an 
optimal molecular shape (para or meta positions), the molecules 
appear to cross the bacterial cell wall more effectively and 
reach their intracellular targets. In contrast, fungal cell walls 
are thicker and more complex, often requiring molecules with 
slightly larger steric bulk or differently oriented substituents to 
interact efficiently with fungal membranes. This helps explain 
why compounds like AZ6 and AZ9 , which possess substituent 
arrangements that increase molecular surface area, show better 
antifungal performance in vitro. 
2.3 
DFT Calculations 
The DFT calculations were carried out to examine the optimized 
structures of synthesized scaffolds, frontier molecular orbitals, 
energy band gaps, MEP, and chemical properties. The fully opti- 
mized geometry of the most potent investigated scaffolds AZ1, 
AZ6, AZ7, and AZ9 is displayed in Figure 3 A–D , respectively. 
Reports suggest that the molecular characteristics and structure- 
activity correlations can be anticipated through the examination 
of frontier molecular orbitals and MEP [ 25–27 ]. Primarily, the 
highest occupied molecular orbitals (HOMO) were found dis- 
persed across the azetidine ring along the central region. In 
contrast, the lowest unoccupied molecular orbitals (LUMO) 
were mainly clustered over the pyridazine ring along with the 
azetidine core in all the molecules (Figure 4 ). As shown in Table 
S12 , these molecules possess a HOMO-LUMO band gap of ∼ 
4.42 eV, displaying their non-conducting nature. Notably, the high 
dipole moment of the synthesized scaffolds AZ1-AZ10 suggests 
favorable interactions with biological systems that carry a high 
dipole character [ 28, 29 ].

Frontier molecular orbitals (HOMO and LUMO) play crucial role 
in determining molecular stability, reactivity, and electron trans- 
fer properties, affecting ionization potential, electron affinity, and 
various chemical descriptors like hardness, softness, electroneg- 
ativity, and electrophilicity. These parameters collectively help in 
predicting how molecules behave in chemical reactions. Herein, 
all insights relevant to the numerous chemical descriptors are 
tabulated in Table S12 . The DFT computation indicates AZ8 , 
AZ9 , and AZ10 molecules possess high-energy HOMO orbitals. 
However, AZ4 , AZ7 , and AZ10 display low-energy LUMO 
orbitals, implying strong bioactivity and a noticeable contribution 
to protein-ligand interaction. Qualitatively, AZ8 , AZ9 , and AZ10 
were characterized by relatively high-energy HOMOs, indicating 
enhanced electron-donating capability, while AZ4 , AZ7 , and 
AZ10 displayed comparatively low-energy LUMOs, indicating 
more substantial electron-accepting potential. AZ6 showed a 
relatively high electron affinity (EA = 1.2321 eV), consistent with 
its predicted ability to accept electron density in protein envi-

revealed that the HOMO orbitals of the more active compounds 
( AZ8 , AZ9 , and AZ10 ) are predominantly localized over the 
electron-rich aromatic ring and heterocyclic core, regions directly 
involved in ligand–protein interactions. This HOMO distribution 
is important because it identifies the principal electron-donating 
sites of the molecule. In the docking study with DNA gyrase 
(5MMN), these HOMO-enriched regions align closely with amino 
acid residues such as Glu50, Ala47, Thr165, and Asp73, which 
are capable of participating in charge-assisted hydrogen bonding 
or polar interactions. The overlap of HOMO density with these 
residues supports stronger donor–acceptor interactions and cor- 
relates well with the superior antibacterial activity of compounds 
like AZ5 , AZ7 , AZ8 , and AZ9 . Similarly, in CYP51 docking

also face the hydrophobic channel leading to the heme core, 
facilitating favorable π–π and π–polar interactions. This explains 
the enhanced antifungal behavior of AZ6 and AZ9 , whose 
HOMO distributions support π-polarizable contacts within the 
CYP51 binding tunnel.

Molecular Electrostatic Potential (MEP) 
The MEP maps facilitate valuable insights into the reactive 
surface regions of the scaffolds, displaying how electronic charge 
is distributed across the investigated molecules. This observation 
helps in identifying potential sites for electrophilic and nucle- 
ophilic attacks, thus offering visual evidence of possible optimal 
binding contacts with biological targets. Herein, the most potent 
scaffold MEP diagrams are shown in Figure 5 . Primarily, the 
red/blue region and green color represents, the negative/positive 
and neutral electrostatic potential, respectively. The blue zone 
signifies the area of nucleophilic attack, and the red zone shows 
the region of electrophilic attack. The fluctuations observed in 
the electrostatic potential are attributed to the electronic envi- 
ronment of the atoms within the molecule. Primarily, negative 
electrostatic potential is noticed near the carbonyl group, and 
the positive potential is identified near -NH atoms emphasizing 
the carbonyl group being an optimal site for electrophilic attack 
and -NH region being more appealing for nucleophilic attack.

Molecular docking was performed to identify an entity that

2.4.2.1 
DNA Gyrase (5MMN) Inhibition. Inhibition of 
E. coli DNA Gyrase B (PDB: 5MMN) is important for drug 
development because this enzyme is essential for bacterial DNA 
replication and cell survival, making it a prime target for antibac- 
terial agents. Among all the studied derivatives, AZ5 expressed 
the most suitable docking profile toward DNA gyrase, with a 
MolDock score of -132.31, re-rank score of -97.81, and resilient 
interactions through hydrophobic contacts and H-bonding (espe- 
cially with Ala47, Ile78, Val43, and Pro79). In a comparable 
manner, AZ8 , AZ7 , and AZ9 also demonstrated considerable 
binding scores (-134.04, -132.67, and -131.68, respectively) with 
numerous favorable H-bonding and hydrophobic interactions, as 
indicated in Figure 6 . Surprisingly, AZ5 and AZ8 both show- 
cased greater in vitro antibacterial activity with MIC values of 
62.5 µg/mL against E. coli . The correlation between low MIC 
and highly negative docking score confirms the notion that 
considerable binding to bacterial DNA gyrase underlies their 
potential antibacterial activity. In contrast, AZ1 demonstrated 
low docking efficiency (-131.69) but substantial inhibition towards 
S. aureus (MIC = 62.5 µg/mL), implying its interaction with the 
enzyme (particularly with Glu50 and Val167) has a significant 
inhibitory function. Notably, all synthesized scaffolds displayed a 
favorable MolDock score when compared to the standard antibi- 
otic chloramphenicol (-105.71) (Figure S56A ). These findings 
support the predicted binding affinity of synthesized compounds, 
further validating the design strategy.

3 
Conclusion 
In summary, a series of metal-free synthesized azetidine pyri- 
dazine derivatives ( AZ1–AZ10 ) were successfully characterized 
and demonstrated moderate to significant antimicrobial activity. 
Among them, AZ5 and AZ8 showed considerable antibacte- 
rial potency against E. coli , while AZ1 , AZ7 , and AZ9 were 
particularly effective against S. aureus . Compounds AZ6 and 
AZ9 exhibited notable antifungal activity toward C. albicans . 
SAR analysis indicated that electron-withdrawing (F, Cl) and 
hydrophobic (CH3 ) substituents enhance biological activity. The 
ADMET evaluation further revealed excellent drug-like profiles, 
high gastrointestinal absorption, BBB permeability, and full com- 
pliance with Lipinski and Veber rules, supporting their potential 
as orally active and CNS-permeable agents. Computational inves- 
tigations reinforced the experimental findings. Docking studies 
showed a strong correlation between binding affinity to DNA 
gyrase and CYP51 and the observed antimicrobial effects. DFT 
analysis highlighted that compound such as AZ8 , AZ9 , and 
AZ10 possess high HOMO energies, while AZ4 , AZ7 , and 
AZ10 display low LUMO energies, indicating favorable electronic 
behavior for protein binding. The rich electrostatic surfaces of 
AZ6 and AZ9 , along with the excellent hydrogen-bonding and
